# Changelog

All notable changes to Tenko DDI are recorded here, one section per release, in the format of [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/) 1.1.0 with Semantic Versioning 2.0.0. One addition: a `### Action required` subsection under a release lists what an administrator must do before or after upgrading to it. Every change to `crates/**` or `ui/src/**` adds an entry under Unreleased; CI checks this with `scripts/changelog.mjs`.

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- Secure dynamic updates with GSS-TSIG (DNS-28, CON-03, TGL-09; RFC 3645, RFC 2930; `docs/dns.md` section 13.11): a Windows client or domain controller, or a Linux host with `nsupdate -g`, negotiates a security context through TKEY with a Kerberos ticket inside SPNEGO or raw, and signs its updates on it; Tenko accepts the ticket with its own acceptor and the keys of its AD computer account. TKEY modes 3 and 5 over TCP, a TKEY query over UDP answered with TC before any work (BADMODE, BADALG, BADNAME), negotiations bounded per client before any Kerberos work and per node by giving up the oldest one waiting, and dropped after 30 s, established contexts bounded by `[dns.gss]` with the idle one leaving first and expiring at the ticket's end or `context_max_secs`; a signed update is verified on its context (BADKEY for an unknown or expired one, BADTIME, a sequence number seen before refused as BADSIG) and every answer is signed. An access list entry of kind `kerberos` allows the principals of a realm (by a name pattern and a prefix), the zone's Kerberos settings name the realms, and `require_tsig` means TSIG or GSS-TSIG. In a zone with `ownership = "windows"`, and in every zone with Kerberos settings under Hardened, names are owned with the Windows rules: the creator owns a new name, members of DnsUpdateProxy leave it unowned, privileged groups by SID from the PAC change any name, locator, wildcard, manual, IPAM and DHCP names are for privileged principals only, an owner with a SID is matched by SID only, a name imported from AD is claimed only by its own host, a removed name is held for its owner for `owner_hold_days`, and TSIG or unsigned updates do not change an owned name (`dns.name_owners`, written with the records). The RRsets record the principal; `dns.update.apply` and `dns.update.refuse` carry the principal, its SID and the ownership decision; refused negotiations are audited as `dns.gss.refuse`, and the findings they point at are raised only from several sources. Metrics `tenko_dns_tkey_total`, `tenko_dns_gss_contexts`, `tenko_dns_gss_establish_seconds` and the label `auth` (`none`, `tsig`, `gss`) on `tenko_dns_updates_total`. The lab round `gss` of `deploy/compose/lab-ad` (E43) and a fuzz target `dns_tkey`.
- Tenko's own Kerberos implementation for secure dynamic updates from Windows clients and domain controllers and for Kerberos sign-in (DNS-28, IAM-13; `docs/plans/identity-plan.md` section 4.2, ADR 0026 point 1), in the new crate `tenko-krb`, with no Kerberos library and `aws-lc-rs` as its only cryptography. This release has its DER codec of the Kerberos structures of RFC 4120 (tickets, authenticators, the AP, AS and TGS exchanges, `KRB-ERROR`, `KRB-PRIV` and the change-password data of RFC 3244) and of the GSS-API and SPNEGO tokens (RFC 2743, RFC 4178): DER only, every length bounded by the input, every refusal naming the structure and the field, the tokens recorded from MIT Kerberos and Samba decoded and encoded back octet for octet, and the fuzz target `krb_der`; and its AES ciphers of RFC 3961 and RFC 3962 (`aes128-cts-hmac-sha1-96` and `aes256-cts-hmac-sha1-96`: the derived keys, string-to-key with the salts of Active Directory user and computer accounts, AES-CTS, encryption with its integrity check in constant time, and the `hmac-sha1-96-aes` checksums), tested against every vector of RFC 3961 and RFC 3962 and the recorded tickets decrypted with their keys; RC4, DES and 3DES are refused; and the key set of the service account, with the selection of a key by a ticket's encryption type and key version, and the import parser of MIT keytab files (format version 2, as `ktpass`, `msktutil` and `ktutil` write them; version 1 is refused with its reason, RC4, DES and 3DES entries are listed and never kept), tested against keytabs written by MIT's `ktutil` and with the fuzz target `krb_keytab`; and the acceptor a secure update or a browser sign-in negotiates with: SPNEGO under both Kerberos OIDs (Microsoft's and the standard one) and raw Kerberos tokens, the `mechListMIC` both ways, the AP-REQ checks of RFC 4120 (the ticket's realm and service, its key chosen by encryption type and key version, its flags and times, the authenticator's client and time within the skew of 300 seconds, the GSS checksum of RFC 4121, mutual authentication required) against the clock the caller gives, a replay cache shared by every negotiation that refuses anything from before the acceptor started, the PAC of [MS-PAC] with its server signature checked with the service key and the SIDs of the user and its groups read from the logon information, and the AP-REP with an acceptor subkey; NTLM-only offers, `NegoEx` and DCE-style requests are refused, and every refusal is typed with the metric outcome and the health finding it points at. Every recorded MIT and Samba exchange is accepted with the recorded clock and refused with a wrong key, an unknown key version, an expired ticket, a skew above the window, another service and a replay; the fuzz targets `krb_spnego`, `krb_ap_req` and `krb_pac` cover it; and the MIC tokens of RFC 4121 that sign every secure update (one HMAC per message under keys derived once per context, the data fed in pieces, compared in constant time), with the recorded MICs verified both ways and the fuzz target `gss_mic`; and the client the controller will use to test and rotate the service account: the AS exchange with `PA-ENC-TIMESTAMP` pre-authentication and the salt of the KDC's `PA-ETYPE-INFO2`, the TGS exchange for a service principal, whose ticket the node's own acceptor then accepts, and the change-password protocol of RFC 3244 to port 464, over UDP with the TCP fallback of RFC 4120 (also on `KRB_ERR_RESPONSE_TOO_BIG`), with a timeout per attempt and the servers the caller lists. It ran against a live MIT Kerberos KDC and `kadmind` (pre-authentication, a wrong password refused, the service ticket accepted, the password changed and changed back over UDP and over TCP) and against a Samba AD domain controller (the adopt flow: a pre-staged computer account's one-time password, the KDC's salt equal to the MS-KILE salt Tenko computes, the password changed, a ticket with the keys derived from the new password, and a user's service ticket accepted with those keys and its SIDs read from the PAC), and those exchanges are recorded as the tests every build runs. Nothing calls it yet; the DNS update path takes it up in a later release.
- The Kerberos account of secure dynamic updates (DNS-28, OPS-09, OPS-11, IAM-16; `docs/plans/identity-plan.md` sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.10, ADR 0026 points 4 and 5). A domain administrator pre-stages one Active Directory computer account with the static PowerShell block of `docs/active-directory.md` section 4.1 (the name, the organizational unit, the `DNS/<fqdn>` and `HTTP/<fqdn>` SPNs, AES only, a one-time password typed at its prompt); every value is read at a prompt and checked, and the block takes nothing Tenko wrote. **DNS, Kerberos** in the web interface, `POST /api/v1/dns/kerberos/adopt` and `tenko dns kerberos adopt` take the account over with that one-time password: Tenko obtains a `kadmin/changepw` ticket with it, changes the password to 120 random printable characters only Tenko knows (RFC 3244), derives the AES keys with the salt the KDC names, reads the key version back from a ticket for the account's own SPN and stores the keys as a system secret of purpose `dns.krb` in the vault, never revealed and never in a file; both passwords are dropped. No directory administrator's credential is ever asked for. A keytab written by `ktpass` or `msktutil` imports instead (`POST /api/v1/dns/kerberos/import`, `tenko dns kerberos import`, the wizard's upload): the AES entries of the realm are kept, RC4, DES and 3DES entries are counted and refused, the file is not kept, and the password stays the administrator's. An adopted account's password is rotated every 30 days by default (TGL-32; the leader service `dns.krb`, the job `dns.krb.rotate`, `POST /api/v1/dns/kerberos/rotate`): the keys of the new password are written to the vault as `pending` before the change is sent, the version the KDC issues is read back, the new keys become current and the old ones previous, and a version stays held until the tickets issued under it have expired (12 hours after the next version became current), so a ticket issued before a rotation is still accepted. A restart, a lost or forged answer or a failed commit between the change and the commit is finished at the next recovery when a KDC takes the new keys; the new keys are dropped only on proof that the change did not happen (an authenticated refusal of the only request, or, 15 minutes after the change, a KDC that refuses the new keys and takes the old ones), never after the KDC confirmed the change. The self-test runs after the adopt, the import and every rotation, daily and on demand (`POST /api/v1/dns/kerberos/check`): an AS exchange with the keys held, a TGS exchange for every SPN with the ticket accepted by an acceptor with those keys, the KDC's clock against the node's. Its findings are kept on the account and shown on the page, in `GET /api/v1/dns/kerberos`, `tenko dns kerberos status` and the health status, each with its fix: `dns.gss.spn_missing`, `dns.gss.spn_duplicate`, `dns.gss.kvno_unknown`, `dns.gss.weak_etype` (tickets of a type other than AES), `dns.gss.clock_skew` (above 60 seconds), `dns.gss.self_test_failed` (a disabled or unknown account, keys the KDC refuses after a reset outside Tenko, no KDC answering) and `dns.gss.rotation_failed` (at the second failure in a row, at the first under Hardened). A finding's text is cleaned of control characters and bounded. The KDCs are the ones listed, or the `_kerberos._tcp` records of the realm in the zones Tenko serves, or the realm's domain name. The audit log records `dns.krb.account.adopt`, `.import`, `.rotate`, `.delete` and `.check` (on a failure or a change) with the realm, the account and the key versions, never a password or a key. `tenko dns kerberos import` gains `--name` for a keytab that names only an SPN. The vault purpose `krb` is declared for the `dns` role of node sync. The secure update path takes the keys up in a later release.
- The Windows DHCP mirror exporter, `deploy/windows/mirror/` (MIG-01, MIG-08; slice 14 of `docs/plans/windows-takeover-plan.md`, the exporter's script line): `Tenko-DhcpMirror.ps1` runs every minute on a Windows DHCP server under an account that is a member of `DHCP Users` and of no privileged group, reads the scopes with their pools, exclusions, lease time, state and DNS settings, the options at server, scope and reservation level with the custom definitions and classes, the reservations and the active leases with the `DhcpServer` module, maps them onto the migration IR as a `MirrorSnapshot` envelope with a loss report (policies, filters, superscopes, failover relationships, offer delays, BOOTP-only scopes, names that are not host names, each with the cmdlet and the object key it came from), and pushes it with `PUT /api/v1/migrate/mirrors/{id}/snapshot` over HTTPS with a token that can write that one mirror and nothing else. ### Security

- Changes that decide the other step-ups need a fresh sign-in (TGL-23, TGL-24, IAM-06, IAM-09): `PATCH /api/v1/settings/security` and `PUT` and `DELETE /api/v1/access/role-policies/{role}` need a session that authenticated within the last five minutes (403 `auth.step_up_required` with `required: fresh` otherwise), as revealing a secret and creating a backup do. Disabling, enabling or deleting the break-glass account needs what designating it needs: a session of at least password and TOTP from the last five minutes. Disabling an account revokes the grants it holds and every grant issued under them, as deleting it does, and the audit record `auth.user.update` counts them in `grants_revoked`; enabling the account again does not bring them back. An admin service account no longer counts as another administrator for the guard that keeps the last one, because it acts through tokens and tokens never administer: the only person with the admin role cannot drop it, be disabled or be deleted while only service accounts would remain (409 `users.last_administrator`). An admin service account itself may be demoted, disabled or deleted whoever else administers.
- A sign-in method or a token outlives the session that adds it, so adding one needs a session that authenticated within the last five minutes (IAM-08, IAM-10): enrolling TOTP, registering a passkey or a security key, and creating a personal, scoped or service-account token (403 `auth.step_up_required` with `required: fresh` otherwise). A token for a service account needs a sign-in that meets the account's requirement as well as its creator's. `POST /api/v1/auth/password` revokes the account's personal and scoped tokens with its other sessions (`tokens_revoked` in the answer and in the audit record) and, with `remove_factors: true`, removes its TOTP seed, passkeys and recovery codes too (`factors_removed`); the password form of the web UI offers this as a checkbox, and an account left without a second factor that its profile requires enrolls one at its next sign-in. An administrator's password reset (`PATCH /api/v1/users/{id}` with `password`) revokes the account's tokens too. A wrong current password counts and is audited as a failed sign-in and leads to the same stored lock, and a stored lock refuses a password change as it refuses a sign-in (429 `auth.throttled`). Setting the first password of an account that has none needs a fresh session. Action required: a script that uses a personal token needs a new token after its owner changes the password or an administrator resets it.
- An account whose source is an external directory has no local password (identity plan section 3.16): `POST /api/v1/auth/password` and an administrator's password reset refuse to set one (400 `auth.invalid_request`, field `new_password` or `password`), and a password sign-in refuses such an account as it refuses an unknown one, also when its record holds a password.
- A token acts only while the sign-in that created it meets its owner's requirement: when a profile, a setting, a role policy or the first operator secret in the vault raises the requirement above the strength the token was issued with, the token is refused with 403 `auth.step_up_required` until a stronger sign-in creates a new one (IAM-06, IAM-10). Action required: after raising a requirement, create the affected tokens again.
- A scoped token reads its owner's sessions, tokens and passkeys (`GET /api/v1/auth/sessions`, `GET /api/v1/auth/tokens`, `GET /api/v1/auth/webauthn/credentials`) only with a grant that reads users (403 `auth.forbidden` otherwise); the same holds for the owner's grants (`GET /api/v1/access/grants`) and the people a grant can name (`GET /api/v1/access/subjects`). A session and a personal token read them as before.
- The authorization index fails closed: when it cannot read an update of a user, a token, the grants or the operator secrets of the vault, it is built again from the store, and when that fails too, that user or token is refused, the grants are dropped and the vault counts as holding an operator secret until a later read succeeds. The error is logged.
- IPAM refuses the nil and the all-ones identifiers for a new object, the custom field definitions included (400 `ipam.validation`, field `id`): authorization reserves both.
- Uploaded import files are read within two more limits and away from the async workers (IPAM-11, API-15): one upload builds at most 5,000,000 cells, each row counting as one, and 64 MiB of text over all its files and sheets, both counted before the rows are built or a value is copied (the shared-strings table of an xlsx before the workbook is opened), so a small xlsx whose rows reach a far column or whose cells all name one long shared string, or many such parts in one request, is refused with 413 `import.upload.too_large`. The readers run on a thread of the blocking pool, two uploads at a time on a controller, so a large upload no longer holds a worker that the API and a DNS or DHCP role in the same process share. The new fuzz target `import_files` covers the readers.
- The DNS transports and the dynamic update intake have deadlines and admission bounds (DNS-05, DNS-07, DNS-11, DNS-14, DNS-17). A write of answers to a TCP, DoT or DoH client has a deadline, `[dns.tcp] write_timeout_secs` and `[dns.tls] write_timeout_secs` (5 seconds, 1 to 60): a connection whose client does not read its answers is closed, its place under the connection caps given back, and the kernel's keepalive probes and, on Linux, its own deadline on unacknowledged data are set to match. The forwarder and the update path hand an answer to a connection without waiting on it: an answer the connection has no room for (64 waiting) is dropped and counted, and the forwarder's in-flight permits are never held on a client's connection. The per-client connection caps `[dns.tcp] per_client` and `[dns.tls] per_client` count an IPv6 /64 as one client, as the rate limiter does. A DoH request has `[dns.tls] request_timeout_secs` (10 seconds, 1 to 60) from its headers to its answer, the body read included (`408` for a body still missing, `504` for a forwarded answer still missing), and a request counts as activity of its connection for no longer than that. Dynamic updates are admitted before they are copied or a task is started for them: at most `[dns.update] in_flight` (256, 1 to 100,000) on the node and `[dns.update] per_source` (16, 1 to 10,000) from one client, an IPv4 address or an IPv6 /64; one over a bound is answered a header-only SERVFAIL at once. An UPDATE over UDP passes the admission of a query first: the parse, the cookie check under `cookies = "required"`, and the response rate limiter. The extended error text of an update refusal is at most 64 octets. The readers of UPDATE and transfer messages grow their record vectors as records parse instead of reserving the counts the header claims. `docs/dns.md` sections 8, 12.6, 12.7, 13.1, 13.5 and 13.9 and `docs/dns-resolver.md` section 7 state each limit and its key; `deploy/tenko.example.toml` carries the new keys with their defaults.
- A forwarding rule with `validate: false` is gated as a negative trust anchor is (TGL-12, TGL-28, DNS-13): `POST /api/v1/dns/forwarders` and `PATCH /api/v1/dns/forwarders/{id}` with `validate: false` need `dns.keys` in an interactive session (403 `auth.forbidden` otherwise, a token included), the root rule `.` never accepts it and the Hardened profile refuses it on every suffix (400 `dns.forwarder.invalid`, field `validate`), and the audit record of the write carries `validate: off` with the rule's id. A rule stored by an earlier version that these rules refuse is listed under `problems` in the node's DNS status (`GET /api/v1/nodes/{id}/dns`) until it is changed or deleted. `tenko dns forwarder create --no-validate` and `tenko dns forwarder update --no-validate` follow the same rules.
- Server-side fetches follow an egress policy (NEVER-01): the URL of a block list and the primaries of a secondary zone are never a loopback, link-local, multicast, unspecified or metadata service address (169.254.169.254, fd00:ec2::254, and the like) unless the new setting `[dns] egress_allow` lists a prefix that holds it; a host name is judged by every address it resolves to, at every lookup, and every redirect of a list fetch (at most five) is judged like the first request. A list URL with credentials (`user:password@`) is refused at the write (400 `dns.list.invalid`, field `source.url`), as is a host written as a refused address; a primary on a refused address is 400 `dns.zone.invalid`, field `source.primaries`. The `last_error_detail` of a list names the kind of failure and the HTTP status, no longer the URL nor the server's answer.
- A zone or an access list that names a TSIG key (`source.primaries[].key`, `transfer.notify[].key`, a `key` entry) is written only by a principal with `dns.keys`, and the key must exist (ADR 0017): 403 `auth.forbidden` and 404 `dns.key.not_found`, as the key routes answer them. A write that names no key needs `dns.write` as before.
- The presentation form of a trust anchor key (`POST /api/v1/dns/trust-anchors`, `tenko dns trust-anchor add`) is read octet by octet (API-15): a DS digest with a character outside ASCII is refused with `dns.trust_anchor.invalid` instead of ending the request without an answer. A test feeds such digests, and the new fuzz target `dns_trust_anchor_text` covers both forms.

### Changed

- Data format 6 (OPS-23): the `migrate` namespace of tables, audit actions and error codes, the object kinds `mirror` and `takeover` and the token scope `mirror:<id>` of the Windows takeover's contracts, and the `kerberos` entry of a DNS access list of the contracts of Slice 13, are values a format 5 binary cannot decode, so the format is raised once for both with an empty migration (`docs/upgrades.md`, `docs/dhcp-contracts.md` section 13, `docs/dns-contracts.md` section 12). The first start of this version migrates the data directory and keeps a copy under `upgrade-backups`; a return to the previous release needs that pre-migration copy. In a controller cluster the migration waits until every voter runs a binary that knows format 6.
- Backups say what file they are, and the audit log has every backup operation (OPS-07, IAM-18, NEVER-02): the audit record `sys.backup.create` of a written backup carries `archive_id` (the identifier in the file's header) and `sha256` (of the whole file), both delivered by the audit export; `tenko backup verify` and `tenko backup restore --dry-run` print the same two values (also in `--json`), so an operator compares a file with the record at the external sink before a restore (`docs/backup.md` section 5); the offline `tenko backup create` prints them when it has written the file. The schedule records the files it deletes beyond `keep` as `sys.backup.prune` (`target_dir`, `keep`, `removed`, `files`), fenced by the leader epoch. A restore writes its notice into the restored store as well as into `restored.json`, so the first start records `sys.backup.restore` (now with `archive_id`, `sha256`, `restored_at` and `notice`) also when the file is gone, and says so (`notice: missing`). Text that comes out of a backup file is printed with control characters replaced, and a manifest with a part name, a version or a key identifier that is not printable is refused as corrupt.
- An MSI upgrade on Windows is adopted by the service itself (OPS-12, OPS-23, OPS-24; ADR 0023, item 11). The installer stops the service, replaces `C:\Program Files\Tenko\tenko.exe` and starts the service; it does nothing in the data directory. The service is registered with `--payload <that file>` and, at every start as `NT SERVICE\tenko`, asks the file for its version; when it is newer, it runs the store check with it (`tenko upgrade check` on a copy of the closed store, the same refusals as by hand), copies it to `updates\<version>\tenko.exe`, points `previous` and `current`, writes the attempt record and stops with exit status 12, so that the new version starts on probation like a version installed from the web UI. A version that refuses the store or a file that cannot be read leaves the running version running and writes an Error to the Application log (`the installer's file was not adopted`); `tenko service start` then fails with exit status 11 and `did not adopt this binary`, and the installer rolls its files back and reports the failure. A file that is not newer is left alone (`nothing to adopt`). `tenko service start` waits through the restart with which the service adopts a file or switches back, and prints which version the layout runs. An upgrade by hand is the same: stage the release as the installer's file and restart the service; the runbook (`deploy/windows/README.md`) has no elevated step inside the data directory anymore. ### Fixed

- The lease view of the observations, when no follower keeps it current (a tool, a test), no longer drops the lease rows that ended longer ago than the observation retention by the day it was built: its readers pass the time they read at, and the retention applies at that time. A reader asking at an earlier time saw no lease facts once the clock had moved past that time plus the retention. The server's followed view is unchanged; it still leaves out at its start the rows past the retention by the clock.
- A controller cluster with one of three voters down no longer refuses writes with 503 `sys.store.no_quorum` under load while the leader and the other follower are up. A linearizable read (IPAM plans it after a stale plan, among other writes) confirmed the leader's leadership with a heartbeat round of its own, and one round that the remaining follower answered later than the heartbeat interval failed the read. The reads that wait at the same time now share one round, and a round a quorum missed is followed by the next one within `[store] read_wait_secs` while the leader's replication is acknowledged by a quorum; an isolated leader still fails at once.
- A secondary zone no longer misses a NOTIFY that arrives while a transfer of the zone runs (RFC 1996 section 4.4): the zone is refreshed again when the transfer ends, unless it ended at the announced serial or a later one, where it waited for the SOA refresh timer before. A catalog zone that its primary changed during its first transfer added its new members only after that timer.
- An update of the audit export settings (`PUT /api/v1/settings/audit-export`) with more than 8 sinks is refused with `audit.export.too_many` before any of its sinks is examined; it was refused after every sink had been checked, with each sink's own problems listed beside the refusal. The batch routes of proposals, the DHCP settings of a pool and the language template accept the same input as before and no longer size a buffer by a count the request carries before that count is bounded. `tenko ca-root --fingerprint` refuses a root with another fingerprint inside the one fetch that accepts any server certificate, as `tenko node enroll` does, so no step of the command holds an unverified root; what the command accepts, prints and writes is unchanged.
- The next page of a list never starts outside the list's own range: a cursor that lies before the first row a list can hold (or, in a list read newest first, after its last) continues from the list's own first row, and a cursor past the list's end gives an empty page. This holds for every list that pages through the store.

### Security

- The Linux installer keeps root apart from the service account as ADR 0023 (I1, I2) requires (OPS-13, OPS-23, OPS-24). Every process it runs as the account `tenko` (`tenko upgrade check`, `tenko setup token`, the copy of the CA root, the removal of the updates layout) starts in a session of its own with no controlling terminal and `/dev/null` as standard input; its output lands in a file of root's and is shown through a filter that keeps printable ASCII only, and a setup token is shown only in the token's own shape (`tenko setup token` and `crates/tenko/src/serve/setup_token.rs` accept the file only in that shape). Root opens no path under the data directory any more: the account reads the CA root out for root's copy and computes its fingerprint, and `tenko health` and `tenko setup` use the copy. An existing `--data-dir` is taken only when it is empty or already the account's own (owner `tenko`, mode 0700); anything else is refused with the reason and what to do (exit 2). `--release-url` and `TENKO_GITHUB_API` must be `https://`, with plain `http://` accepted only for an address of the loopback interface, and every `curl` call restricts the protocol of the request and of every redirect (`--proto`, `--proto-redir '=https'`); `deploy/proxmox/ct/tenko.sh` does the same for what it fetches. The GitHub token comes from `TENKO_INSTALL_TOKEN`, `GITHUB_TOKEN` or `--token-stdin`; `--token` still works and warns that it is deprecated. `--uninstall` removes `<data_dir>/updates` as the account, with `rm --one-file-system`, only when it is the account's own directory, and `--purge` removes the data, configuration and log directories as root with `--one-file-system`, both only once no process of the account runs (otherwise they are left, with the reason). `bash install.sh --setup-token` prints the setup token that way, and `deploy/proxmox/ct/tenko.sh` reads the token of a container through it (`--setup-token <ctid>` prints it again). The runbooks of `docs/upgrades.md` (sections 7.3 and 9.7) and `deploy/README.md` follow the same pattern: a command run as the account from root's prompt uses `setsid` and `/dev/null`, and root removes nothing under the data directory.
- Every answer of the API and the web interface carries `Permissions-Policy` and `Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy: require-corp` beside the `Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy` and `Cross-Origin-Resource-Policy` it carried before (TGL-08, ADR 0008; the findings SF-0008 and SF-0009 of the security report of 0.0.10). The permissions policy denies the browser features the interface never uses (camera, microphone, geolocation, payment, USB, serial, Bluetooth, screen capture, MIDI, the motion sensors, fullscreen and the rest of the standard list) and allows the clipboard and the passkey ceremonies to the interface's own origin only. The embedder policy, with the opener policy, keeps the interface in a browsing context of its own, so a page of another origin cannot hold a reference to it or embed its answers and read them through a Spectre-class attack; it costs nothing, because the interface loads no resource from another origin. The ZAP rules 10063 and 90004 of the release round fail the round from now on when a header is missing, instead of reporting a finding. Nothing changes for an administrator or an API client.
- Scoped authorization checks every prefix a write places below another (IAM-01): creating, resizing or restoring a prefix around existing prefixes is a change to each of them, so the principal's grants must cover each of them where it is and where it goes; otherwise the write, and its dry run, answers 403 `auth.out_of_scope` and changes nothing. A create or resize whose new network holds a prefix outside the grants is refused before planning, so the answer names nothing of that prefix. Principals without scoped grants are not affected (`docs/access.md` section 3.3).
- Releasing a DHCP lease (`POST /api/v1/dhcp/leases/{addr}/release` and `POST /api/v1/nodes/{id}/dhcp/leases/{addr}/release`, scope `dhcp.write`) needs `execute` on the DHCP scope that holds the lease (IAM-01); a lease in a scope the principal may not read answers 404 `dhcp.lease.not_found`, as reading it does, and nothing is released. Principals whose grants reach every DHCP scope are not affected (`docs/access.md` section 3.3).
- A DHCP client's claim to a name is checked against every record at the name, whatever its type and address family (DHCP-13, DNS-01; RFC 4703 section 5, ADR 0020 point 2): a name that holds records of another origin (`manual`, `ipam`, `dynamic`, `transfer`, `ad`) of any type is refused to a client whose DHCID does not stand there, for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 alike, and a name IPAM gives one of its addresses is refused at every other address, before and after the DNS actuator wrote the host's records; `ipam` records give way only where the zone's `may_replace_ipam` allows it, as before. A client whose DHCID stands at the name keeps updating its own records beside data an operator added later. Under `replace`, a client that takes a name over from another client takes the other client's records of both families. The zone service applies the same rule at the write: a `dhcp` change adds nothing at a name that holds data of another origin unless a `dhcp` DHCID already stands there (`dns.record.origin`). The lease shows the refusal (`holds <type> records of the origin <origin>`, `IPAM manages <name>`), counted in `tenko_dhcp_ddns_total{outcome="refused"}`; `docs/dhcp.md` section 13.4 has the table.
- A DDNS name is written into the zone that holds it (DHCP-13; ADR 0020 points 3 and 6): a name below the forward zone's apex that lies in a child zone Tenko serves, or below a delegation of the forward zone, counts as a name outside the forward zone and is cut to its first label under it, or refused with `refuse_foreign` (`lies in the zone <zone>`, `lies below the delegation <name>`), so the forward zone never holds a child zone's name. A removal looks for the client's DHCID in the zone that holds the name and then in the zones above it, so a name an earlier version wrote into a parent zone is removed on release and expiry.
- The release path is tightened in what an installation and a user of the container image get (API-19, in part; L1.4, L1.6, L1.9 and L1.10 of `docs/plans/security-audit-plan.md`, section 3). The container image `ghcr.io/linkztream/tenko:<version>` is built from the released Linux binary itself, the file the smoke runs started, the security round tested and the provenance attestation names, on a `distroless/static` base pinned by digest, instead of from a second build of the binary inside the image on base images taken by tag; it reaches the registry only after the security round and the attestations of the release files passed, pushed by digest, and the tags `<version>` and `latest` (stable releases only) are set when the release is published, so no image of a release that failed sits behind a tag. The image's provenance and SBOM attestations name that digest, and the image's SBOM is taken from the image before it is pushed. The release compiler is the exact version `rust-toolchain.toml` names (1.99.1, the version 0.0.11 was built with), bumped in its own commit, instead of whatever the stable channel serves on the day of the run; the web UI's dependencies are installed without their install scripts and the whole dependency tree is audited before the bundle is built. `SHA256SUMS` lists `releases.json` beside the other files, so a signature or attestation of `SHA256SUMS` covers the manifest too, and every earlier release that `releases.json` carries over is checked against the `SHA256SUMS` its own release published before it is kept. A release is made from a tag only, whose commit must be on `main`; the security round runs from that commit. Once a certificate is configured, the Windows binary and the MSI are signed in jobs that hold the signing secrets and run nothing but the signing script, with the password never on a command line; the MSI is built from the signed binary and smoke-tested after it is signed. `deploy/Dockerfile` keeps its build of the web UI and the binary for local development behind the build argument `TENKO_BINARY_SOURCE` (default `source`); the published image uses `release`.
- The offline commands never run with full privileges over another account's files (ADR 0023 items 3 and 11, invariants I1, I2 and I10; OPS-07, OPS-13, OPS-23): `tenko backup restore`, `tenko backup create` of a stopped node, `tenko backup verify`, `tenko kek rotate`, `tenko upgrade check` and `tenko upgrade layout` refuse to start as root (on Windows: as `SYSTEM` or with an elevated token) when this binary's own file, the data directory, or the directory the command writes into (`--out`, `--work-dir`) belongs to another account. The command exits with status 2 and prints one line with the code `exec.unsafe_owner`, the path and its owner, and the same command in the form that runs it as that account (`runuser -u tenko -- tenko …`); there is no override and no profile setting. On a node installed by `deploy/install.sh` these commands run as `tenko` (`docs/backup.md` section 3, `docs/secrets.md` section 8). A data directory that root owns itself, as in a container that runs as root, passes.
- The restore and the offline backup create everything under the data directory themselves (ADR 0023 I2; OPS-07): directories in one step with mode `0700`, files as new files with mode `0600`, and a path that already exists as a symbolic link where the restore creates `store`, `restored`, `restored.json` or its work directory is refused with `backup.io` before anything is written through it. `tenko backup verify` keeps its scratch store in a directory of its own with mode `0700` and removes it on every way out, a panic included. A backup file's manifest bounds every part before it is read (64 KiB for the key generations, 1 MiB for the TLS files and the configuration, 64 GiB for the snapshot), and the store's snapshot reader bounds the header, each key and the bytes a compressed snapshot expands to by declared limits, so a crafted or damaged file fails cleanly instead of filling memory or disk. The plain bytes of a backup pass through buffers that are wiped when they are dropped while a file is written and read, and the passphrase of an online `tenko backup create` is wiped once the request has been sent.
- The Windows service keeps one privilege (OPS-10, OPS-12; ADR 0023, item 11): `tenko service install` sets the service's required-privileges list to `SeChangeNotifyPrivilege` alone, so the token the Service Control Manager builds for `NT SERVICE\tenko` holds none of the rights a service account carries by default; `sc.exe qprivs tenko` shows the list, and the smoke tests assert it. `service install --account` refuses `LocalSystem`, `NT AUTHORITY\LocalService` and `NT AUTHORITY\NetworkService` with `service.account_shared` and exit status 2; the virtual account is the default and another virtual account is accepted. The MSI's firewall rules are bound to the service (`Service="tenko"`, the service SID), so no other program on the host gets the ports they open.
- Nothing elevated writes into the Windows data directory after the first installation (OPS-10, OPS-12, OPS-23, OPS-24; ADR 0023, I2 and item 11). `tenko service install` looks at the data directory before it registers anything: a new or empty directory is restricted to Administrators and SYSTEM first, the version layout is written into it while the service account cannot write it, and the account is granted last; a directory that holds data is left as it is when its owner and its access list are already the ones `service install` sets for this account (the line `left as it is`), and refused otherwise (`service.data_dir_in_use`; a link or a junction at the path with `service.data_dir_link`; data without a version layout with `service.data_dir_no_layout`). The MSI no longer runs `upgrade check` or `upgrade layout` on an upgrade; see the change above.
- The listen windows of the scanner's passive methods are bounded, so what a probe holds during a window no longer depends on how much the senders on its segment send (SCAN-02, SCAN-03, SCAN-05; row A1 of `docs/plans/security-audit-plan.md`): a run of `mdns` or `llmnr` keeps what the answers say about at most 4,096 addresses, with at most 64 service ports per address, and leaves out addresses outside the scope as it reads each answer instead of at the end of the run; a window of `lldp` keeps at most 512 neighbors; a window of `dhcp_snoop` reports at most 2 host names per address and at most 16,384 sightings. A segment a probe covers fits in each bound. What a window drops is counted and named in a note of the run. Text read from LLDP, CDP and DHCP frames (system names, port ids, descriptions, the CDP platform and software version, DHCP host names and vendor classes) loses control characters and invisible direction and format marks through the one cleaning the scanner applies to text from the network, which DNS answers and SNMP replies go through as well (SNMP text now also loses the direction marks, DNS text is now also trimmed), and `tenko node scan run` and `tenko node scan status` print such a character in a name, a description, a note or an error escaped (`\u{1b}`) rather than raw. Tests cover each bound, the counts and notes, and the cleaning; the fuzz target `scan_dns_message` merges its answers into a listen window. The limits are in `docs/scanning.md`, sections 2.5, 2.6, 2.11, 2.12 and 7.2.
- The resolver's denial cache (RFC 8198, RFC 8020) and its validator check more before they trust a record (DNS-13, DNS-34). The signer: an NSEC or NSEC3 record, an SOA and a wildcard RRset are kept under the zone whose signature the validator checked them with (the deepest signer of their RRSIGs that encloses the owner), only when that zone is the zone of the answer's SOA, and only with that zone's signatures; the validator also takes the zone of a denial proof from that signer, never from the first RRSIG's signer field, and an NSEC record whose next name lies outside its zone is not kept. The labels bound: an RRSIG whose labels field is below the label count of its signer's name is never accepted, a wildcard RRset is kept only with labels at or above its zone's, and an NSEC wildcard proof must cover the name with the closest encloser the RRSIG's labels name (RFC 4035 sections 5.3.1 and 5.3.4); a record of a delegation or a DNAME above the wildcard it covers proves nothing below it. The negative TTL cap: the denial cache keeps an SOA, and so synthesizes a negative answer, for at most the answer cache's `negative_ttl` (3,600 s by default). An NXDOMAIN that is not `secure` marks a name absent (RFC 8020) only when its SOA is unsigned, and from a forwarder only for the question name, not the end of its CNAME chain; such an answer never replaces the records of a zone held from secure answers. Fewer names are answered from the denial cache than before; nothing changes for clients otherwise.
- A sign-in counts as one attempt for the account's lockout (IAM-05, IAM-06, TGL-07): a wrong TOTP or recovery code counts toward the lock like a wrong password, and a correct password alone no longer clears the account's failures; they clear only when the sign-in completes with its last factor. The lock of the profile (10 failures under Standard, 5 under Hardened) is therefore reached by wrong passwords and codes together, across any number of challenges, with the `auth.user.lock` audit record and, under Hardened, the alarm. A locked account is also refused at the second-factor step.
- Each sign-in, TOTP enrollment and passkey registration challenge is recorded as used in the store in the same write that completes it (IAM-06): a used challenge is refused by every controller and after a restart, not only by the controller that saw it. The passkey step of a password sign-in spends its second-factor challenge when it starts.
- An xlsx upload is read cell by cell, and a value beyond the row or column limit (200,000 rows, 256 columns) refuses the file as too large before the sheet is built (IPAM-11); the size a sheet declares is not trusted. xls and ods workbooks are refused with "the file cannot be read" until they can be read within the same limits; save them as xlsx or CSV.
- A revoked node loses its connections on every controller (OPS-04). A controller closes the connections of every node it finds on the revocation list, also when the list was empty before (the first revocation of an installation, and the first after the hourly maintenance emptied it) and for the revocations already on the list when it starts; before, a node revoked through another controller in those cases kept its open configuration, command and heartbeat streams there. Every open configuration, command and heartbeat stream of a node also checks once a minute that the node is still enrolled and holds the certificate of its connection, and ends with the refusal a new call would get (`cluster.node.unknown`, `cluster.node.certificate_unknown`) when it no longer does.
- A node certificate is renewed only when it is due, at most once an hour, and a renewal pushes the replaced certificate out (OPS-04). The controller refuses `Node.Renew` before the certificate's renewal window (a third of its lifetime, or the larger of 30 days and the CA's `renew_before_days`, before its expiry, the rule the node itself asks by) with 409 `cluster.node.renew_not_due`, and a second renewal of a node within an hour with 409 `cluster.node.renew_too_soon`; `tenko node renew` reports the same before the window. A renewal keeps two certificates in the node record, the new one and the one the node renewed with, and drops any other, so the record and a revocation stay small however often a node renews; before, every renewal added a certificate valid for a year. The certificate the node renewed with stops working 15 minutes after the node shows that it holds the new one (it connects with it, or its heartbeat reports the new expiry): calls with it are then refused with 503 `cluster.node.certificate_replaced`, and the node connects again with the new certificate. A node whose renewal answer was lost keeps its old certificate and renews again an hour later; the heartbeat answer asks a node to renew at most once an hour. A revocation covers the retired certificate too until it expires, and the hourly maintenance pass cuts a node record that still names more than two certificates back to two, so such a node stays revocable in one write.
- The `Raft` service between controllers refuses a revoked controller with 403 `cluster.node.unknown` (OPS-04). It accepts a caller without a node record because the first controller of a cluster never enrolled; a revoked controller has no record either, and a certificate of it that the revocation list does not name (one its record no longer held) passed that check. The controller's node id on the revocation list now decides.
- Refused enrollments no longer write an audit record each (OPS-04, NEVER-02): a controller records the first 10 refusals of a minute one by one, as before, and 10 more of requests that knew a token's secret (a used or expired token, a refused role or name), and the rest of that minute as one `cluster.node.enroll` failure at its end, with the code `cluster.enrollment.refusals` and how many requests were refused, how many of each code, from how many source addresses, and the first 16 of them seen. A flood of invalid requests to `POST /api/v1/nodes/enroll` from the network no longer fills the audit log and the store at the rate limiter's pace.

### Added

- A release says what it is made of and where it was built (API-19, in part; SA-E3 of `docs/plans/security-audit-plan.md`): it carries a CycloneDX SBOM for the Linux binary and the Windows binary (`tenko-<version>-<platform>.cdx.json`, the crates the release build compiles, from `Cargo.lock`), for the web UI that both embed (`tenko-<version>-ui.cdx.json`, its production dependencies) and for the container image (`tenko-<version>-image.cdx.json`), each listed in `SHA256SUMS`, and a SLSA build provenance attestation for every release file (the binaries, the MSI, the SBOMs, `install.sh`, `tenko-proxmox.sh`, `releases.json`, `SHA256SUMS`) and for the image digest, plus an SBOM attestation that binds each SBOM to its binary or the image. The attestations are signed through Sigstore's public instance and are checked with `gh attestation verify <file> --repo linkztream/tenko` (`SECURITY.md`, "Verifying a release"). They prove that the project's release workflow built a file, not that the owner released it; a signature that a node checks before it installs is still to come. The release stops when the web UI has a production dependency with a high or critical advisory (`npm audit`), and every action of the release workflow is pinned to a commit.
- Every release is checked by a lab round `security` on the binary being released, before it is published (SA-E4 and SA-E6 of `docs/plans/security-audit-plan.md`): it installs a throwaway node from that binary, scans the ports it answers on and its TLS, checks the systemd unit and the service account, sends malformed DNS to it, sends every operation of the API that is not public by design (`deploy/security/rules/public-operations.txt`, each with its reason) without a session or a token and expects a refusal, and scans the web interface and the API with ZAP's passive rules as a read-only account and token that the round removes afterwards, with one `PASS`, `FAIL` or `SKIP` line per check and the evidence encrypted to the owner's key. A check that fails stops the release, except the two ZAP scans, which report their findings until their rule file has been through two releases. A public summary of what each release's checks found is published under `docs/security/` (`release-<version>.md`), with the full report kept privately until a finding is fixed (`SECURITY.md`, "Checks at every release").

### Changed

- Zone files are read and written by Tenko's own reader and writer (DNS-01, MIG-15, DNS-24): the import (`POST /api/v1/dns/zones/{id}/records/import`, scope `dns.write`, and `tenko dns import`) and the export (`GET /api/v1/dns/zones/{id}/records/export` and `tenko dns export`) no longer use the zone file scanner of the DNS library, which stays for the wire format only. An error names the exact line of the token where it was found, also inside a record that spans lines, and a quote left open at the end of the file names the line that opened it; a record after a directive carries its own line. TTLs, `$TTL`, the SOA timers and the RRSIG original TTL take BIND's units (`1h30m`, `2d`, `1w`). Every type reads the generic form of RFC 3597 (`\# 4 c0000201`), and a record of a type Tenko has no presentation form for (LOC, URI and the like) written in its own form is reported as a loss with its line instead of stopping the import. SVCB and HTTPS read and write the parameters of RFC 9460 (`alpn="h2,h3"`, `no-default-alpn`, `port`, `ipv4hint`, `ech`, `ipv6hint`, `dohpath`, `ohttp`, `keyNNNNN`); the export wrote `alpn` as raw octets and `no-default-alpn` as `nodefaultalpn`, which no reader took back. A TXT string longer than 255 octets is split into strings of at most 255 octets, as the API splits it, and text with octets outside printable ASCII is read as it is. The export writes the same text as before, except the SVCB and HTTPS parameters and data a type's form cannot carry (an empty DS digest, DNSKEY key, RRSIG signature or TLSA data, and opaque data that does not parse as its type, which made the export fail), now written in the generic form. A differential test against the previous reader, a round trip of every type through the export and the fuzz target `dns_zonefile` with new seeds cover the reader.

## [0.0.10] - 2026-10-31

### Changed

- The store's snapshots follow the entries, not the size of the log (OPS-26, DHCP-17, DNS-11; ADR 0030): `[store] snapshot_every_log_mib` is a sanity bound of 1,024 MiB by default instead of a trigger of 64 MiB, which fired every 600 entries once a lease commit's entries had grown and wrote a full copy of the state every few seconds (the storms of the lab's DHCP and DDNS runs). A due snapshot waits up to 30 s while the commit queue is deep, and the log line `store snapshot built` names its duration and the wait. The Raft log records and the snapshot files are compressed on their way to disk (raw DEFLATE): a snapshot of 4.17 MB of rows is 501 KB. A node of this version sends snapshots that an earlier version cannot install; the members of a cluster are upgraded together. The coalescer's group commit packs at most 128 batches and 64 KiB into one Raft entry instead of 1,000 and 8 MiB.

- The DHCP role writes no IPAM observation facts; the lease tables are the source (DHCP-16, IPAM-21, IPAM-23, OPS-26; ADR 0031): `dhcp.leases` and `dhcp.leases6` are the observation source `dhcp:<node>`, and a view over them presents each lease as the address, name, OS and vendor facts it stands for (a conflict as the answering MAC address, a delegated prefix as a network fact). The reconciler, the move step, the reconciliation view, the findings, the observation API and the topology read the leases through that view, joined with the stored facts of the probes, the network controllers and the zones. The view runs in the IPAM service of every node that runs the IPAM module and keeps one entry per lease in memory (185 bytes inline, about 300 with the host name, the vendor class and the OS guess: about 30 MB at 100,000 leases and about 300 MB at 1,000,000 on a controller), is built from the lease tables at start, follows the store's change stream off the apply path and is built again after a snapshot install; a reader waits at most one second for it to reach its own read view (after that it reads the view as it is, and the request never fails), and the reconciler's light run is woken after the view took a lease change in. Fact rows of a `dhcp:*` source that earlier versions wrote are no longer read and leave by the retention (30 days after their last sighting); the view applies the same retention, so a lease whose facts ended longer ago than that is no longer presented (ghost addresses and "seen since" age out as before) and the lease rows themselves are never deleted; no new one is written, and an ingest of a `dhcp:*` source is refused. The metric `tenko_dhcp_lease_facts_total` is removed. The observation id of a lease fact is now a hash and changes once at the upgrade: an open proposal's evidence shows the new ids after its next refresh, and no proposal, supersede or rejection depends on them.

- The lease history row is lean, and the history read says where the host name comes from (DHCP-07; ADR 0031): a `dhcp.history` row holds the event, the state after it and its end, the scope, the client identity, the node and the lease row's revision in about 60 bytes (the key and value together 85), no longer a copy of the host name, the fingerprint and the DDNS state. Rows of earlier versions still read. `GET /api/v1/dhcp/history` joins the host name from the lease row while it still has it, and each item gains `names`: `lease` (the row is still the one the change wrote), `lease_now` (the row changed since, for the same client), `unknown` (another client holds the address or the row is gone) or `stored` (an old row holds the name itself). The DHCPv6 history is unchanged and keeps the whole event. History rows written by this version are not readable by an earlier version, and an earlier controller does not see the leases of a newer node; upgrade the members of a cluster together.

- The lease row and the lease API carry the grant time (DHCP-07, IPAM-21; ADR 0031): `granted` is the time the current binding was first granted. A renewal, a rebind or a repeated request of a live binding keeps it, and so does a release, a decline or an expiry of the same client; a new grant after the binding ended (the same client's released or expired row, or an active row past its end) and a grant to another client set a new grant time. `GET /api/v1/dhcp/leases` items gain `granted` (a row without it reads as granted at the client's last transaction), and "seen since" in the IPAM view comes from it. The field is additive and the store's data format stays 5; the field `observed_at` is no longer written.

### Fixed

- The metric `tenko_dhcp_derived_queue` reports the number of lease changes waiting for the asynchronous writer (OPS-26). It was published through the setter of a mirrored counter, which stored the count as the bits of a floating-point number, so a queue of 11,311 changes read as a value a few hundred orders of magnitude below one and the series was useless for the writer's health; a test reads the gauge back.
- The lease commit's write amplification (DHCP-07, DHCP-16, DHCP-17, OPS-26; ADR 0030): since 0.0.3 every lease change carried its IPAM facts in the batch that the ACK waits for, an address fact with two index rows, a name fact with two more, an OS and a vendor fact, and the lab measured 1,996 exchanges per second for one worker (9,990 in 0.0.2) and a commit average of 16.5 ms at 1,000 per second (2.6 ms). A lease commit now holds the lease row and its client index entry only: 2 rows and about 670 bytes instead of 13 rows and 3,714 bytes for a client with a host name, a parameter request list and a vendor class. The history row follows within one second from an asynchronous writer per family (the facts are a view of the lease table, see the change of the observation source above), every 250 ms in batches of 16 KiB that the store's coalescer carries in a background class, one beside the lease commits of each entry, so a lease commit never waits behind the derived rows, and never holds an ACK; a full queue drops the row, counts `tenko_dhcp_derived_dropped_total` and raises the health finding `dhcp.role.derived_dropped` after a second. The start rebuilds what a crash lost from the lease table (an earlier change of the same address within the lost second keeps no history row). The log written per lease commit fell from 3,738 bytes to about 60, the entries from 415 KB to about 5 KB on average under load. New series `tenko_dhcp_derived_written_total`, `tenko_dhcp_derived_rebuilt_total` and `tenko_dhcp_derived_queue`. The gate guard `bench/phase3-dhcp/scripts/guard.sh` holds the commit average, the closed-loop rate and the store bytes per lease to `bench/phase3-dhcp/guard.toml`.

- The store work per lease change falls from 13 rows to 3 (DHCP-17, DHCP-07; ADR 0031): a lease commit still writes 2 rows (669 bytes, 61 bytes of log), and the derived work is the history row alone, 1 row and 125 bytes instead of 11 rows and 3,048 bytes (the writer's log bytes per lease fall from 357 to 26). A renewal writes the lease row, no client index entry and one history row. 3 rows (2 and 1): the 2 rows of 0.0.2 plus the history row that 0.0.2 did not keep. The asynchronous writer of the entry above kept up with only about 4,000 changes a second in the lab, because the node applies about 65,000 rows a second at most.

### Security

- The libraries that read an uploaded spreadsheet are updated: `zip` (with `calamine` and `rust_xlsxwriter`, which moved to it) fixes parser mismatches, unbounded handling of symbolic-link targets, a denial of service in legacy compression methods, files written outside the extraction directory and an unchecked integrity code on encrypted entries in archives, and `seroval` (the web interface's serializer) fixes script and code injection through crafted regular-expression sources and typed-array constructors. Administrators who import spreadsheets are protected against hostile archives inside the uploaded file.

A locked host never gets a proposal that would change it, and an unmanaged host gets none.
- Adoption policies per prefix, inherited by the prefixes inside it, that approve discovered hosts without a person: optionally only inside DHCP pools, only for listed vendor prefixes of the MAC address, or only after a number of sightings.
- Operator secrets: passwords, API keys, SSH keys and tokens with a name, purpose tags, a user name and notes, kept encrypted in the vault, each under a key of its own. Administrators create, change, replace and delete them; operators see them and may reveal them. A secret that a host or a connector still uses cannot be deleted, and its references are listed.
- Revealing a secret shows it once and copies it to the clipboard, which is cleared again after 30 seconds; every reveal, and every refused one, is written to the audit log with the person, the host and the reason, never the value.
- Credentials of a host: secrets bound to a host as its administration, remote desktop, SSH or web login, with "Copy password" on the host page.
- Remote access from the host page: a Remote Desktop file or an SSH link for each address of a host, with the user name of its credential and no password in either.
- The security profile toggle TGL-24 decides what a reveal takes: the signed-in session (Home, Lab), a sign-in within the last five minutes (Standard), or a passkey sign-in within the last five minutes and only from the page of a host the secret is bound to (Hardened).
- Probe nodes: `tenko node enroll` enrolls a node with a controller using a single-use enrollment token, which an administrator creates in the API or with `tenko node enroll-token`. The node checks the controller's CA fingerprint, keeps its key to itself and receives a certificate with its node id and roles; `tenko serve --roles probe` then runs it. The node connects to the controller over mutual TLS on the cluster port (8444 by default, opened once a node or a token exists), sends heartbeats, renews its certificate before it expires, and keeps its observations in a buffer on disk while no controller is reachable, sending them in order once one is. `tenko node status` shows the node and its connection, `tenko node renew` renews at once.
- The node registry: `GET /api/v1/nodes` lists the enrolled nodes with their roles, certificate, version and live connection, enrollment tokens can be listed and deleted, and revoking a node closes its connections and refuses its certificate from then on. `GET /api/v1/system/status` reports enrolled and connected nodes and their versions.
- Controllers and nodes agree on the protocol version, so they can be upgraded one at a time; a node that shares no version with its controller is refused with a clear error and shown as a warning in the system status.
- A `[cluster]` section in `tenko.toml`: the addresses of the cluster port, the endpoints nodes are told, the heartbeat interval, the token lifetime and the size of a node's buffer.
- The probe scanner: a probe node discovers devices in the prefixes of the `[scan]` section of its `tenko.toml` and sends what it sees to the controller, each method on its own schedule. Without raw sockets it reads the ARP and IPv6 neighbor tables of its host, sends ICMP echo, scans a list of TCP ports and notes which answer with TLS, looks up the names of the discovered addresses in reverse DNS, asks mDNS and LLMNR for names, services and operating system hints, and polls switches over SNMPv3 (SHA-1 or SHA-2 authentication, AES-128 privacy) for their ARP, bridge and LLDP tables, so clients appear with their switch port and VLAN and switches with their links. Vendors of MAC addresses come from a built-in OUI table.
- Raw-socket scan methods on Linux with `CAP_NET_RAW`: `arp_sweep` (an active ARP sweep of the scope), `tcp_syn` (a half-open SYN port scan that can list up to all 65,535 ports), `os_fingerprint` (an OS family guess from the SYN-ACK's TTL, window and options), passive `lldp` (LLDP and CDP neighbors as switch-port, VLAN, link and management-address facts) and passive `dhcp_snoop` (DHCPv4 DISCOVER, REQUEST and ACK, reporting each client's address, name, vendor class and server). They are off by default; each needs its config section enabled, also on a probe's scan configuration page, which shows their timeouts, listening times, the CDP switch and the interface override. On Windows they answer `unsupported`, because raw sockets need Npcap, which Tenko does not bundle.
- The vendor table of the scanner holds the whole IEEE MA-L registry (40,316 blocks as of 2026-10-10), so every MAC address with a registered manufacturer gets a vendor.
- Scan safety: scans never leave the configured prefixes and their exclusions, run within a rate limit per probe (TGL-19: 100, 50 or 10 probes a second, or unlimited) and a spacing per address, stay out of blackout windows such as `mon-fri 08:00-17:00` in a chosen time zone, and active probing can be switched off (TGL-18). `tenko node scan stop` is a kill switch that stops every running scan at once until `tenko node scan resume`.
- Probe configuration from the controller: each probe's scan configuration is kept by the controllers (`GET`, `PUT /api/v1/nodes/{id}/scan-config`) and synced to the probe as deltas, or as a compressed snapshot after a long absence, within a bandwidth cap per stream (`[cluster] sync_kbps`); the probe applies it atomically, keeps it across outages and reports its sync state and scope. A probe without one offers its own `[scan]` section as the initial configuration; once synced, the controller's configuration wins.
- Scans on demand: `POST /api/v1/scans` scans a prefix, a host or an address with the probes that cover it (passive refresh, port scan, SNMP poll) as a job with progress, and stores the diff against the facts before it (new, changed and gone per fact type).
- The kill switch per probe and installation-wide from the API (`POST /api/v1/nodes/{id}/scan/stop`, `/resume`, `POST /api/v1/scans/stop`, `/resume`): it stops running scans within seconds, reaches an offline probe when it connects again and shows when the probe confirmed it.
- SNMP credentials of probes are named as operator secrets and fetched over mutual TLS when a probe needs them, with an audit record each time; they are kept in memory and never stored on the probe.
- `tenko node scan status` shows the methods with their schedules, state, last run and what the host allows; `tenko node scan run <method>` runs one method at once, for a single address or prefix if wanted, and `--dry-run` prints what it saw instead of sending it.
- Observations name the address a device has a name for (reverse DNS, mDNS, LLMNR, SNMP) and the vendor of a MAC address, and open TCP ports note which answer with TLS; the observation view lists names, vendors and TLS ports per device.
- Discovery in the web UI: an inbox of the proposals, filtered by state, kind, severity, network and device and updated live, with the evidence behind each proposal and a preview of the changes it would make; approve it, reject it with a reason, dismiss a conflict once its cause is resolved, or run the reconciler at once. The observations of every source can be browsed and looked up per MAC address, IP address or name, with what each source reports, the matching hosts and addresses, and the proposals about the device.
- The host page shows what the sources report about the host: the facts per source, its open TCP ports, its operating system, when it was last seen, and the open proposals about it.
- The adoption policy of a prefix in the web UI, on the prefix's Adoption tab: the policy in force and the prefix it comes from, set for the prefix or cleared so that it inherits again.
- Connectors in the web UI: the installed plugins with their capabilities and platforms, and the connector instances with their health, plugin process and last run. A form built from the plugin's own settings creates and changes an instance, with secrets chosen by purpose (never shown) and writes off unless switched on; instances can be run or checked at once, enabled, disabled and deleted.
- Nodes in the web UI: the enrolled nodes with their roles, connection, versions and certificate expiry, revocation, and enrollment tokens that are shown once together with the command to run on the node. A node that shares no protocol version with the controller is pointed out.
- The start page shows the open proposals, the healthy connectors and the connected nodes.
- VLANs with their networks: creating a VLAN, optionally with a network prefix, first previews the IPAM changes and the network controller's read-back of the network; the job then creates the VLAN and prefix, writes the network through the controller after a dry run, checks it by reading it back, and rolls everything back when a step fails. Without a network controller, with writes switched off for it (TGL-20) or on request, the network becomes a manual step of the job. The management VLAN is never written. An existing VLAN can be put onto the controller the same way, and the prefixes of a VLAN are listed (`POST /api/v1/vlans/provision`, `GET /api/v1/vlans/{id}/prefixes`).
- The topology map: routers, switches, access points, hypervisors, virtual machines, containers and hosts with their wired ports, wireless associations, LLDP links, uplinks and virtual machine placement, computed from network controllers, SNMP probes, inventories and the IPAM data and filtered by site, VLAN, network, kind and text; each device shows its ports and what is connected to each port, the path to the gateway, and links to its host and observation pages (`GET /api/v1/topology`, `GET /api/v1/topology/nodes/{id}`).
- The Proxmox VE connector (plugin `proxmox`, bundled): it reads a cluster read-only through an API token (the role PVEAuditor on `/` is enough): the nodes as hypervisors, and the virtual machines and containers with their node, MAC addresses, IP addresses from the QEMU guest agent or the running container (else the static addresses of the configuration and of cloud-init), operating system, status, pool, tags, bridge or SDN vnet and VLAN, so they appear as discovered hosts and on the topology map under their node. Filters by node and pool, stopped guests and templates on request, large clusters read in pages.
- The Home Assistant connector (plugin `hass`, bundled): it reads the device registry read-only through a long-lived access token over the REST and WebSocket API: devices with their MAC addresses and the IP addresses their entities show, the manufacturer as the vendor of each MAC address, the model, the integration and the area, and device trackers that belong to no device. Devices without a network address, Bluetooth and Zigbee identifiers, the stale address of a tracker that is away and public IPv4 addresses (unless asked for) are left out; integrations can be included or excluded.
- Both connectors check the server certificate against the system's certificate authorities, a pinned CA or a pinned fingerprint, can connect to a fixed address while checking the certificate against the host name of the URL, name the cause when TLS fails and show the fingerprint the server presents; turning the check off is loud in every health check and log line.
- For plugin authors: inventory sources can report the vendor of a MAC address (vendor facts, field `vendors` of the inventory protocol), and the Rust SDK has a client for web APIs with the certificate checks above, error mapping and bounded reads (`tenko_connect::sdk::http`).
- For plugin authors: the Rust SDK has an anonymizer for recording the answers of a real system as fixtures (`tenko_connect::sdk::anonymize`): addresses become documentation addresses with networks and relations kept, identifiers keep their format, names become placeholders, credentials and locations are dropped. It splits the property strings of a Proxmox VE guest configuration so that their MAC address, bridge and VLAN tag stay readable, and narrows a private network to the blocks seen in it when the documentation and benchmarking ranges cannot hold it at its real width, and writes a bare prefix length (`netmask`, `prefix`) as the width of the anonymized network of the address next to it. The UniFi, Home Assistant and Proxmox VE plugins ship such recordings of a real controller, instance and node next to their documented fixtures, with a recorder in their live tests.
- The UniFi Network connector (plugin `unifi`, bundled): it reads a self-hosted Network Application or a UniFi OS console through the classic API with a local account: switches, access points and gateways with their ports, port profiles, uplinks and LLDP neighbors, the connected clients with their switch port or access point, wireless network and VLAN, the networks with their VLANs and DHCP mode, the port profiles, and the wireless networks with their VLANs and the access points that broadcast them. Devices and clients also arrive as inventory hosts with names and vendors, so they appear on the topology map and as discovered hosts. Without a local account, a local Integration API key gives a reduced read-only view of devices, uplinks and clients. The controller's certificate is checked against the system's certificate authorities, a pinned CA or a pinned fingerprint (the health check shows the fingerprint a self-signed controller presents).
- UniFi writes through the connector, each read back and diffed first, previewed as a dry run, verified by reading back afterwards and off unless the connector's writes are switched on (TGL-20): a VLAN-only network for a VLAN, or a new name or DHCP mode for the network that has it; a port profile on a switch port; a per-client VLAN override. Each write carries the steps that undo it. Uplinks, the switch port and the client through which Tenko or the controller is reached, and the management networks are never written.
- The observation views of the web UI filter and show the name and vendor facts, and the picture of a device lists its vendors and the TCP ports that answered with TLS.
- Moves change the network of each device through the network controller: the switch port of a wired device gets a port profile that carries the target VLAN untagged, and a wireless device gets a per-client VLAN override. Each change is read from the controller again when the job reaches it, refused when the device or its port changed since the move started, written after a dry run and verified by reading back. When the device is not seen at its new address in time, the change is undone with the controller's undo steps. A device the controller cannot move gets a manual step that says exactly what to do and why: no controller, not seen by it, writes off (TGL-20), a port shared with other devices, no port profile for the VLAN, an access point that does not carry the VLAN (join the named wireless network instead), or a guardrail.
- The move preview shows the network change of every device: the port profile on which switch port, the VLAN override on which access point, or the manual step and its reason, with the change on the controller, its side effects and the wireless networks that carry the target VLAN. A device on an uplink, on the port through which Tenko or the controller is reached, or that is the controller itself is refused by a guardrail, shown with the rule and left to a person after an acknowledgment; the other devices of the move are not held up. A move starts only the plan that was previewed, and asks for a new look at the preview when the network changed in between.
- The jobs view shows the network step of a move with the change the controller read back, when it was written and verified, the undo steps kept for a rollback, and the rollback itself.

- Grants within a scope (IAM-01): a person or a service account can hold the operator or viewer role over a VRF, a site or a prefix, with or without what lies below it, and then sees and changes only the objects inside: lists, searches, the prefix tree (which shows the ancestors of a granted prefix as context rows with the network only), exports, observations, proposals, jobs, the topology, the audit log and the event stream are filtered, and anything else answers 403 `auth.out_of_scope`. - The DNS engine (Phase 2): an authoritative server for the zones in the replicated store, answering over UDP and TCP on IPv4 and IPv6 from an in-memory index that follows every change without stopping answers. It answers with wildcards, CNAME chains, DNAME, referrals with glue and negative answers with the SOA, selects views by client subnet, transport, address, type, time and name, hands names outside its zones to a forwarder or refuses them, and supports EDNS (UDP size 1232), DNS cookies (RFC 7873, RFC 9018) and extended errors (RFC 8914). UDP runs on one worker thread per core (one socket per worker on Linux, a shared socket on Windows); TCP pipelines queries with an idle timeout and caps per node and per client. The query path allocates no memory, and `docs/dns.md` describes it.

- The DNS resolver side (Phase 2): names outside the zones go through a cache to upstream resolvers chosen by forwarding rules (`/api/v1/dns/forwarders`, `tenko dns forwarder`), by longest suffix and view, with a root rule as the default forwarder, and to the name servers of stub zones, learned from their source servers. - DHCP fingerprinting and the reconciliation view (Phase 3, DHCP-14, IPAM-21, IPAM-23): a lease's parameter request list (option 55) in the client's order, its vendor class (option 60) and whether it sent options 77, 81 and 93 are matched against a rule table written in the project (38 entries: Windows, macOS, iOS and iPadOS, Android, Linux with dhclient, systemd-networkd, NetworkManager and BusyBox udhcpc, ChromeOS, consoles, printers, lwIP devices such as ESP8266, ESP-IDF and Tasmota, and PXE, UEFI and iPXE boot firmware), exact lists before list prefixes before the vendor class alone, each with a confidence; the guess becomes an OS observation of the lease and option 60 a vendor observation. The new page IPAM → Reconciliation, `GET /api/v1/ipam/reconcile` and `tenko ipam reconcile [--prefix] [--host] [--kind]` show one view over intent, leases, DNS records and scans: ghosts (intent nothing has shown for longer than the scope's lease time), rogues (leases or sightings without intent, with the discovered-host proposal), orphans (DNS records of an address no host holds), PTR mismatches and conflicts (two MAC addresses on one address), each with the truth that wins: intent for managed hosts, the lease for dynamic hosts, the newest sighting without intent, never automatically for a conflict. The host page shows the operating system by precedence (an inventory's statement, then an active scan's guess, then the DHCP fingerprint's) and the vendors, and the system status warns of lease conflicts (`ipam.lease_conflict`). See `docs/dhcp.md` section 15 and `docs/reconciliation.md` section 11.
- The contracts of Tenko's own recursive resolver (ADR 0021): trust anchors and negative trust anchors as rows (`dns.trust_anchors`, `dns.ntas`), the resolution mode of views and forwarding rules, the validation states with their wire rules, the engine's interfaces with in-memory references, the IANA root trust anchors (key tags 20326 and 38696) and root hints shipped in the binary and named by `tenko --version`, the metric families, the `[dns.recursion]` settings (checked, not used yet), the routes under `/api/v1/dns/trust-anchors`, `/api/v1/dns/ntas` and `/api/v1/nodes/{id}/dns/recursion` and `.../infra` and the commands `tenko dns trust-anchor`, `tenko dns nta` and `tenko dns recursion` (they answer "not implemented" until the resolver lands), and a Recursion placeholder page in the DNS section. The resolver's cache now keeps one entry per name, type and class whatever the client's DO and CD bits, asks upstreams with DO, and gives the DNSSEC records only to clients that ask for them; the cache browse shows each entry's validation state instead of the DO and CD bits. See `docs/dns-contracts.md` section 11.

### Changed

- The version is 0.0.2 (every crate, the UI package and the OpenAPI document), the first version a node can upgrade to from 0.0.1 through the installer or the updates page.
- Switching to the Hardened profile, or setting the audit export to `required`, is refused with 409 `audit.export.required` until at least one export sink is enabled; the refusal is audited like the PIN coupling rule.
- The API rate limiter and the login throttle count per controller: behind a load balancer over several controllers a client gets that many times the configured rate and free sign-in attempts before the replicated account lock takes over.
- The API names the health of a connector's remote system `RemoteHealth`, apart from the liveness answer of the health route, so generated clients see the three health values.
- Credentials taken over by an import are operator secrets bound to their host; the login becomes the user name of the secret. Credentials imported earlier are taken over at the first start of this version.
- Operators may reveal operator secrets, so the PIN rule applies to operator accounts too: once the vault holds a secret, an operator signs in with a password, a second factor or a passkey.
- A move completes its observation step by itself when the device is seen at its new address from its own MAC address; a person can still confirm the step by hand.
- The operator secrets page moved under Settings, to `/settings/secrets`, as a tab beside Security, Users and Updates; the former `/secrets` address and the links to one secret keep working and redirect there.
- A bulk move runs four devices at a time by default, instead of two; the preview still offers 1, 2, 4 and 8.
- `GET /api/v1/openapi.json` answers the committed OpenAPI document byte for byte, in the same pretty-printed form that `tenko openapi` prints, instead of compact JSON.
- `tenko serve` starts in eleven steps: a new `cluster` step after the store, and the step of the node transport is now called `nodes`.
- The data-directory format is 3. The first start of this version migrates the data directory (after the usual copy), and earlier versions refuse it afterwards; to go back, restore the copy taken before the migration.
- `tenko kek rotate` refuses to run on a member of a cluster.
- A user may hold no global role at all: grants within a scope can be their only access.
- The prune of the node registry runs on the leader only (`cluster.prune`).
- The web UI shows inventory actions only to those who may take them: viewers and the auditor no longer see the write actions of the IPAM, host and job views, and a person with scoped grants sees them only on objects inside the scope. The server still decides every request.
- A canceled job whose cancellation undid the steps that had run shows as "Rolled back (canceled)".
- A step that has to be undone by hand names the step in words instead of its internal kind.
- In right-to-left languages the arrow keys of the grids and of the prefix tree follow the reading direction.
- The systemd unit reads the key-encryption key with the `systemd_credential` provider instead of as a plain key file, so the warnings about a plain key file are gone, and it creates `/var/log/tenko` for the log file.
- While a start migrates the data directory, `tenko serve` asks systemd for more time, so a long migration no longer hits the start timeout of the unit.
- The Linux release binary is statically linked (musl) and runs on any distribution; every release is started once in Debian 12 and in a RHEL 9 compatible container before it is published.
- The Linux release binary allocates memory with jemalloc instead of musl's own allocator: zone imports and DNSSEC signing run about three times faster, NSEC3 signing of a million records finishes in under two minutes, and the idle memory is unchanged. Building it from source now also needs `make`.
- The container image and the Compose examples publish the cluster port 8444.
- `tls = insecure` on a connector is accepted under the Lab profile only: elsewhere it is refused (`connect.tls.insecure_refused`), and an instance stored with it before the profile changed is shown degraded and is not called until its TLS settings change.
- In a controller cluster the connectors are polled on schedule by the leader only; every controller still runs the plugins, their health checks and the runs a person asks for.
- An import with credential columns is refused, like storing a secret by hand, while another account that may reveal secrets signs in with a PIN alone; the preview names the accounts.
- The monospace font is served with a content-hashed name and cached for a year, like the other build output; it was revalidated on every page load before.
- DNS metrics cost less on the query path: each UDP worker counts on a metrics shard of its own and adds its query counts and latencies to the shared metrics in batches (every 128 queries, a second after the first, and when it is idle), so a scrape may see a UDP query up to a second late; release builds are optimized as one unit, which makes the Linux binary about a fifth smaller and an idle node about 6 MiB smaller, and takes longer to build.

### Security

- The cluster port accepts only node certificates of the installation's internal CA that are not revoked, and a node accepts only a controller of its own CA. Enrollment tokens are kept only as a digest, work once and expire after an hour by default; every enrollment, and every refused one, is written to the audit log. On Windows a node's private key is sealed with DPAPI.
- A PIN session never reveals a secret, and personal API tokens never do. The vault takes no operator secret while an account that may reveal secrets signs in with a PIN alone.
- Reveals are limited to ten a minute per person, counted across all controllers of a cluster.
- A plain, unprotected key generation placed beside generations sealed with DPAPI or systemd-creds is not loaded unless `[kek] allow_plain = true`; the refusal raises an alarm and an audit record.
- A controller that starts with a weaker key protection than it had before raises the alarm `vault.kek.downgrade` and keeps a warning in the system status.
- Connectors can be given a secret only by people who may change secrets, so the reveal policy cannot be bypassed through a connector.
- Repeated requests with an idempotency key are recognized by a fingerprint keyed with a random secret that exists only in memory and changes at every start, so the stored fingerprints reveal nothing about the requests; sign-in, setup and password requests are never fingerprinted. A retry after a restart of a request sent before it is answered as already performed instead of being run again.
- Before a rollback undoes a write through a network controller (a VLAN network or the network change of a move), the undo steps are checked against the guardrails on a fresh read of the controller, so an undo never changes an uplink or the path through which Tenko or the controller is reached.
- The one-time setup token of a first start no longer appears in the log or in the output of a service. It is kept in `setup-token` in the data directory, readable only by the service account, until setup is complete; `tenko setup token` prints it, and a start in a terminal still shows it on the console.
- A token for the role `controller` needs an interactive sign-in within the last five minutes and lives at most an hour; the installation's key encryption keys reach a joining controller only sealed to a one-time key of that host, once per token, audited as `vault.kek.transfer`.
- The controller services of the cluster port accept only controller certificates of the installation, and a data directory refuses to start under another node id.
- A scoped token never exceeds its owner: its grants must lie inside the owner's when it is created, and every request is checked against the token's and the owner's grants as they are then, so revoking an owner's grant or role takes the right from the token at once. The token lifetime cap (TGL-05) is checked again at every use, so lowering it shortens existing tokens.
- Grants and scoped tokens are kept in tables that earlier versions do not read, so going back to an earlier version refuses them instead of widening them into global access.
- Routes refuse a scoped principal unless they decide how the object is scoped, and a scoped write is checked before it is planned and again object by object on its plan, so neither an answer, an event nor an error names an object outside the principal's scope.
