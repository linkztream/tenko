# E22: M26 to M28 of the shipped 0.0.8 and of its fix, two lab VMs over the host's bridge, with the regression's diagnosis (musl)

Verdict: DOCUMENTED, and E22 is met on the final build: with WP9.6 and WP9.7 (ADR 0030 and ADR 0031) the static musl binary of `72a20803`, measured with ISC's perfdhcp from the load VM to the node VM over the host's bridge with every lease committed to the store before its ACK, reaches 9,995 4-way exchanges per second within 0.1 percent drops at one worker and the same at two and four (9,994 and 9,995; 0.0.2 at `a5f9416` on the same VMs: 9,990), 10,789 to 11,125 per second closed loop with the engine's own client, and at 1,000 per second a p99 from REQUEST to ACK of 23.0 ms (p50 3.0 ms), the server's commit p99 at most 25 to 50 ms, clear of the fallback line of P3-Q1 (fewer than 1,000 per second sustained, or a p99 from REQUEST to ACK above 100 ms at 1,000 per second) by a factor of ten in rate and four in latency. The shipped 0.0.8 reached a fifth of that rate (0.41 percent lost at the 2,000 rung) because WP8.1 put the lease's IPAM facts into the batch the ACK waits for (section 2).

M26 asks for the 4-way exchanges per second with every lease committed before the ACK, M27 for the renewals per second, M28 for the p99 from DISCOVER to ACK and the commit's share of it (plan section 6). E22 is the rerun of [2026-10-22-m26-m28-perfdhcp.md](2026-10-22-m26-m28-perfdhcp.md) on the final build (deviation 180 of `docs/plans/phase-3-exit.md`). It found the shipped 0.0.8 at a fifth of 0.0.2's rate; this file documents the regression, the bisect that names its cause, the fix in two packages (WP9.6, [ADR 0030](../../../docs/adr/0030-durability-classes-of-the-lease-path.md); WP9.7, [ADR 0031](../../../docs/adr/0031-the-lease-table-is-the-observation-source.md)) and the fixed build's figures. Version 0.0.10 is cut from `72a20803` plus this file. Every figure is a single-node figure (plan section 2.12): one server process on the node VM, perfdhcp on the load VM over the host's bridge. The runs from the workstation that came first (section 5) are not E22's figures: they are what showed that the workstation's load path costs most of the packet rate and that the regression is in the store work per lease.

## 3. The cause and the fix

**The cause.** WP8.1 made the `dhcp` role write the IPAM observation of a lease at commit, with no polling, as the plan's decision P3-Q8 asked, and it put those facts into the batch the ACK waits for: for an office client an address fact with two index rows, a name fact with two more, later an OS fact and a vendor fact, each a full observed fact, beside the lease row, its client index entry and the history row. For an office client the batch grew to thirteen rows and 3,714 bytes (the cap test below), the Raft entries that group commit builds from such batches grew with it (415 KB on average in the cap test's burst of 1,000 commits), and under load the 64 MiB log bound then fired a full snapshot every few hundred entries inside the commit stream (every 277 to 563 entries at the 5,000 rung, section 1). The commit average at 1,000 per second went from 5.3 ms (the parent) to 26.5 ms (WP8.1), and the ceiling from about 10,000 to about 2,000 per second.

**The fix.** WP9.6 introduces two durability classes of the lease path ([ADR 0030](../../../docs/adr/0030-durability-classes-of-the-lease-path.md), Accepted 2026-10-30; the ACK rule of ADR 0019 and DHCP-17 unchanged). The lease commit holds the lease row and its client index entry only, and the ACK still leaves after that batch is durable. The history row and the IPAM facts, built with the lease row against the same table state, follow within a second from an asynchronous writer: a queue of 65,536 changes, flushed every 250 ms or at 1,000 waiting, in store batches of at most 64 KiB, each its own Raft entry; a full queue drops and counts (`tenko_dhcp_derived_dropped_total`, the health finding `dhcp.role.derived_dropped`), and the start rebuilds what a crash lost from the lease table. The snapshots are due by entries (`snapshot_every_entries`, 10,000) and `snapshot_every_log_mib` becomes a sanity bound of 1,024 MiB; a due snapshot waits up to 30 s while the commit queue is deep. The group commit is bounded at 128 batches and 64 KiB per entry (it was 1,000 batches and 8 MiB). Raft log records and snapshot files are compressed on their way to disk.

**The second step.** WP9.6 protected the ACK path but left the ceiling at 5,000 per second (section 4): every lease change still wrote 13 store rows, 2 synchronous and 11 derived, and the node applies about 65,000 rows a second at most, a rate the writer shares with the lease commits ([ADR 0031](../../../docs/adr/0031-the-lease-table-is-the-observation-source.md), Context). WP9.7 (ADR 0031, Accepted 2026-10-31; it amends ADR 0030, ADR 0019 point 3 and the plan's decision P3-Q8) removes the rows instead of scheduling them: the lease table is the DHCP observation source, so the `dhcp` role writes no fact rows; the history row is lean (about 60 octets; the cap test holds its key and value at 85 octets, about 250 with the host name before), the grant time `granted` is in the lease row, and the view ages rows out at the observation retention. `dhcp.history6` keeps the whole event for now. A renewal writes the lease row without an index move, plus the history row.

The cap tests of the two packages (`crates/tenko-dhcp/tests/lease_write_cost.rs`: 1,000 concurrent commits of an office client; the figures are the tests' on the workstation, as ADR 0030, ADR 0031 and the test file record them, not lab figures; each cap is the measured value plus 25 percent):

From the workstation the same example on either VM is clean only up to the 5,000 rung (4,992 to 4,993), loses 0.80 to 1.01 percent at 10,000, mostly DISCOVERs, and 4.32 percent at 20,000; its average DISCOVER to OFFER is 0.65 to 0.78 ms at 10,000, ten times the VM-to-VM figure, and its closed loop reaches 0.55 to 0.60 of the VM-to-VM rate. The path through WSL2's mirrored networking and the Hyper-V virtual switch costs a quarter of the clean rate by rung (4,993 against 19,965 per second) and about half in the closed loop, before any store work; it does so on both VMs, so neither VM nor its storage is the cause. The workstation path is good for a smoke run and nothing else, and E22 runs on the two lab VMs.

DNS was checked the same way, VM to VM with the shipped 0.0.8 against the 0.0.2 build of `a5f9416` on the same VMs, two runs each: the update path (M21, signed dynamic updates through the store, 60 s at 1,000 and 30 s at 5,000 per second) and the query path (M17, dnsperf, one worker). ## Caveats

- The two VMs share one hypervisor's CPU, which is why the procedure of 2026-10-26 had moved the load to the workstation. The 0.0.2 build at 9,990 per second and the engine at 19,965 per second on the same two VMs the same afternoon show that the sharing does not limit these rates; observed on the host during the runs: 89 percent idle, a load average of 2.3 on 28 threads. A node on its own hardware with a local NVMe disk will do better.
- The node VM's disk is a qcow2 on an NFS share, as on 2026-10-22 (0.25 to 1 ms per flush). The figures are those of this storage; a slower flush path costs latency (section 5: 68.6 ms p99 at 1,000 per second on the zvol, 815 ms on the NFS disk with stalls).
- The probes of sections 1, 2 and 4 have one run per rung and stop after the first rung that loses more than 1 percent; their ceilings are rungs of a coarse ladder (1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000). The 0.0.8 probe did not offer the 1,000 rung. The full ladder with two runs per rung is at the end of section 4.
- The 0.0.2 figures of today are a build of `a5f9416` made on 2026-10-30 with the release recipe, not the binary copied out of the `tenko:lab` image on 2026-10-22; both builds give the same ceiling.
- The fixed build was probed on 2026-10-31, the day after the 0.0.8 and 0.0.2 runs, on the same VMs, disk and topology; the 0.0.2 figure it is held against is the one of 2026-10-30, measured the same afternoon as 0.0.8's. The binary of `72a20803` reports itself as 0.0.9; 0.0.10 adds this file only, so its code is the code measured here.
- The `dd` baseline and the earlier flush loop are shell measurements of different patterns (a new file written synchronously against appends and in-place overwrites); they bound the flush rate, they do not measure the store's own fsyncs.
- The server's commit averages and snapshots come from each rung's `server-*.log.metrics` and `server-*.log` in the raw sets; the histogram buckets are coarse ("at most 100 ms" is anything from 50 to 100 ms). The snapshot rows compare builds at the same entry count, not per lease: the logs do not give the lease count at a snapshot. The Raft entry size and the store's file sizes of section 1 were read on the node VM's disk after the day's runs and are not in a raw file.
- The workstation's NIC negotiated 1 Gbit/s on a 2.5 GbE switch; it is not in the VM-to-VM path, and at these rates (under 60 Mbit/s at 5,000 exchanges per second) it limited nothing in section 5 either.
- "With and without metrics" is still not measured: neither `tenko serve` nor the example server can turn the `dhcp` role's counters off (M33, deviation 176).
- Run 1's disk was on NFS by mistake and the ramdisk run has no durability; neither may be quoted as a Tenko figure. Section 5 as a whole is not E22's figures.
- Kea's and ISC's published figures compare only with the caveats of plan section 6 (`bench/phase3-dhcp/README.md`): other hardware, Kea's threading settings, other perfdhcp options, and a memfile backend whose lease write is not a replicated commit. The DNS reference points of Phase 2 do not apply.
- Plan section 2.12 stands: every figure is a single-node figure.
