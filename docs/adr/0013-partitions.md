# ADR 0013: Partitions and multi-site ownership

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-10-11

## Context

ADR 0014 made the controllers one Raft group: a write commits only through a quorum of voters, and every member reports a quorum state (`single`, `healthy`, `electing`, `isolated`). OPS-26 asks what a controller does when it is cut off from that quorum: it must say so, refuse writes with a retry hint, keep serving reads with a staleness indicator, let the user keep a change for later, and on reconnection re-plan what was kept and either apply it or show its conflict, with nothing merged silently. Data-plane nodes (`dns`, `dhcp`, `probe`) must keep working from their local state (CONTRIBUTING rule 5), and their observations must reach the cluster once a path exists. OPS-27 (Phase 5) asks for sites that keep changing their own objects while cut off. The decision Q10 of 2026-10-09 announced this ADR; S3-Q13 decided how queued changes are treated after a heal.

## Decision

1. **The control plane never diverges.** There is one Raft group for all controllers. A member without a quorum cannot commit, so after a partition heals there is nothing to merge in the store: the minority catches up through the log or a snapshot (ADR 0014). Multi-master replication, last-writer-wins and CRDT merges of intent are not used.
2. **The isolated mode.** A controller whose quorum state is `electing` or `isolated` is in the isolated mode. Every API answer it gives carries `Tenko-Staleness: since=<RFC 3339>; revision=<n>` (when the state began and the revision of its local copy). Reads keep serving the local copy. A quorum gate in the API, after authentication and before idempotency and planning, refuses every mutating request with 503 `cluster.no_quorum`, `Retry-After`, and the parameters `since` and `queueable`; a dry run (`?dry_run=true`) passes, because it writes nothing. A `single` or `healthy` controller adds nothing.
3. **The local queue.** On an isolated controller a person can queue an IPAM intent write instead of losing it: the creates, changes, deletes, restores and bulk changes of tenants, VRFs, sites, VLANs, prefixes, ranges, addresses, hosts and interfaces, the writes that go through the IPAM planner. Imports, settings, users, secrets, connectors, allocations of the next free address or prefix, moves and proposals are not queueable: their outcome depends on state the isolated controller does not have, or they are not intent. The queue is local to the controller and durable: one file per item under `<data_dir>/queue/`, with the request, its translation into planner commands, the plan computed against the local copy, the principal, the time and the session. An item expires after 7 days. The queue is not replicated, because a cut-off controller cannot replicate anything; only its own controller drains it.
4. **Heal.** Once the controller accepts writes again, it drains its queue after a linearizable read (it has caught up). Each item is planned again against the current state and checked with the author's current rights (a disabled account, a revoked token, a lost role or scope refuses it). An item whose plan is unchanged (the same objects, actions and field changes) applies as an inline job with the principal `System { component: Ipam, on_behalf_of: author }` and the audit context `queued_at`, `queued_on` and `original_session`, under an idempotency key derived from the item, so a drain interrupted after the commit replays instead of applying twice. Every other item, and every expired one, becomes a record in the table `ipam.queued_changes` with its conflict (the code and detail of the refusal, or both plans), which the author re-applies or discards. Applied items are recorded there too, so every applied item is listed to its author: nothing applies silently.
5. **Observations.** A controller that cannot write refuses `Observations.Ingest` with `cluster.no_quorum`; the probe keeps the batch in its disk buffer and tries the next controller, so a probe on the minority's side delivers to the majority when it can reach it, and to any controller after the heal. The in-process scanner of an isolated controller hands its batches to a local buffer. Facts merge per source as in `docs/reconciliation.md`; observations are facts about the world, not intent, so they need no queue.
6. **Data plane.** `dns` and `dhcp` nodes serve from their synchronized local configuration whatever the controllers' state (OPS-28). DHCP lease state across a partition is merged by the DHCP failover protocol with MCLT (Phase 3), not by this mechanism.
7. **Multi-site ownership later.** A site that must keep changing its own objects while cut off gets a site-local Raft group that owns its prefixes, scopes and zones (OPS-27, Phase 5), with global objects read-only at an isolated site and explicit ownership moves. Until then the local queue is the only way to keep a change made on the minority's side.

## Consequences

- An isolated controller is honest: every answer says how old its data is, and no write can half-succeed on it. Clients (UI, CLI, scripts) can recognise the state from one header and one error code.
- The queue costs disk on the controller (bounded per item and in count) and a drain after each heal; a controller that never heals keeps its items until they expire, and they are reported as expired after the next heal.
- A queued change is applied by the system on behalf of its author, so the audit trail names both and the queue context; the author sees every outcome in one list and decides on every conflict.
- An item is re-planned, not replayed: identifiers chosen at queue time are kept, but allocations and positions are computed against the state after the heal, and anything that changed what the plan would do turns the item into a conflict instead of a surprise.
- Changes that are not queueable must be repeated by hand after the heal; the 503 answer says whether the request was queueable.

## Alternatives considered

- **Refuse without a queue.** Simplest, and correct, but OPS-26 asks for a way to keep a change, and a person working at the cut-off site would lose their work.
- **Queue as intent proposals.** Proposals are the reconciler's suggestions from observations (ADR 0005), with their own lifecycle and deduplication; mixing user writes into them blurs both. A separate table keeps the two apart.
- **Every queued change waits for confirmation (S3-Q13 b, c).** The most conservative, but it turns every partition into manual work; the owner accepted automatic application of unchanged plans.
- **Replay the HTTP request after the heal.** Replaying runs the original request against a changed state and applies whatever that produces; re-planning and comparing the plans is what lets the author see a conflict instead.
- **Writable minorities with a merge (multi-master, CRDTs).** Intent such as "this address belongs to this host" does not merge without conflicts that a person must resolve anyway, and divergent stores would break the audit chain and every precondition of ADR 0011.
