# ADR 0030: Durability classes of the lease path

- Status: Accepted (points 1 to 5 amended by ADR 0031)
- Date: 2026-10-30
- Amends: [ADR 0019](0019-dhcp-data-model-and-lease-path.md) point 3 (what one lease commit writes) and the plan's decision P3-Q8 (the IPAM observation in the batch of the lease change); ADR 0002 decision 4 (the coalescer's bound) and condition 4 (the snapshot policy)

## Context

DHCP-07 asks for a durable lease store, committed before the answer, with lease history; the phase 3 exit (`docs/plans/phase-3-exit.md` section 2, M26 to M28) holds the node to the figures of 2026-10-22, 9,990 exchanges per second for one worker and a commit average of 2.6 ms at 1,000 per second (DHCP-17). On 2026-10-30 the lab measured the shipped 0.0.8 VM to VM with perfdhcp and found 1,996 exchanges per second and a commit average of 16.5 ms at 1,000 per second (357 ms at 5,000, queueing). The fsync cost of the disk and the packet path were the same as before; the store work per lease was not:

| | 0.0.2 | 0.0.8 |
|---|---|---|
| store rows per lease (snapshot `rows=` against the leases) | 1.8 | 5.2 |
| bytes per Raft entry | small | about 82 KB, about 29 leases per entry |
| snapshots during 100,000 leases | 2, every 10,000 entries | 3 in 25 s, every about 600 entries (82, 152, 209 MB) |

A bisect named WP8.1 (bbd79a3c, "lease observations at commit"): its parent measures 9,987 exchanges per second and 2.2 rows per lease, WP8.1 itself 2,000 and 5.3. WP8.1 had followed P3-Q8 to the letter: the IPAM facts of the source `dhcp:<node>` went into the batch of the lease change, an address fact with its `BY_MAC` and `BY_IP` index rows, a name fact with `BY_IP` and `BY_NAME`, and with WP8.2 an OS and a vendor fact, each a full `ObservedFact` (source and subject keys, an observation id, five timestamps, sighting counters). On the shape of an office client (a host name, a parameter request list and a vendor class), one lease commit grew from 3 rows to 13 and to 3,714 bytes of CBOR (`crates/tenko-dhcp/tests/lease_write_cost.rs`). The entries grew with them, the snapshot policy's log bound of 64 MiB fired every 600 entries, and every snapshot copied the whole state into the disk the log's fsyncs need.

None of the rows WP8.1 added is read on the path that answers a client. The DORA path reads the role's in-memory table; the history is read by people and by the retention; the facts are read by the reconciler, which runs at most every few seconds and waits for its periodic run for anything but a waiting move.

## Decision

1. **Two durability classes.** The lease row is the only thing committed synchronously before the ACK, with the client index rows it moves (`dhcp.leases.by_client`, which the role's own table and a rebuild read): DHCP-07 and the ACK rule of ADR 0019 point 4 are unchanged. Everything derived from the row is **eventually consistent within one second**: the history row (`dhcp.history`, `dhcp.history6`) and the IPAM observation facts of the source `dhcp:<node>` with their index rows. They are built with the lease row, against the same table state, in a second batch, and written by an asynchronous writer once the lease committed. They are never lost after the lease is acknowledged, except by a crash inside that second (point 3).
2. **The asynchronous writer** (`tenko_dhcp::lease::derived`). Each family's committer pushes the derived batch of a committed change into a bounded queue (65,536 changes) before it completes the ticket: a lock and an append, never a wait, so the ACK is never held by it. A flusher writes everything queued every 250 ms, or at once when 1,000 changes wait, in waves of up to 32 store batches of at most 16 KiB each, proposed at once in the store's background class (point 10). Two batches in flight together never write the same row: a change that would ends the wave, so a later change of a row lands after the earlier one. The facts index takes a fact it already holds from the commit without reading it back. A store that refuses keeps the queue and the flusher retries every interval. (The lab's two probes of 2026-10-30 led here: one 64 KiB batch per store round trip wrote 2,200 changes a second and dropped history above that; waves of 32 batches of 256 KiB in the foreground kept up but put up to 8 MiB of rows ahead of a lease commit, 306 ms of commit average at 5,000 a second.)
 A push to a full queue drops the change's derived rows and counts them (`tenko_dhcp_derived_dropped_total`); a queue full for longer than one second raises the health finding `dhcp.role.derived_dropped` until a flush succeeds. Shutdown flushes the queue. The facts index (`LeaseFacts`) takes the facts of a committed change at once (`ObservationHook::committed`), so a change that follows before the flush (a release and a new grant of the address within 250 ms) is judged against them and not against the store; an operator's release flushes the writer before it reads the row, so a flush can never write an older fact over the release.
3. **A crash: rebuilt from the lease table, not a persisted queue.** At start the role rebuilds what a crash may have lost (`derived::catch_up`, `catch_up6`): every lease row whose client transaction time is at most ten seconds older than the newest history row (all rows when the history is empty) gets its history row when it is missing (the row's state read as the event that leads to it; an active row reads as a grant), and its facts by the rules of `observe::ingest`, which write nothing for a fact that is stored. A persisted queue would be correct too, but it is a second synchronous write per lease, the cost this record removes; the lease table holds the facts' whole input, and the history's newest row bounds the rows to look at to a few seconds of changes. What the rebuild cannot give back is an earlier change of the same address within the lost interval: its lease row was replaced and its history row stays lost. The crash test (`tests/lease_crash.rs`, 10 kills under a load in which every one of 200 clients changes every 40 ms) loses no acknowledged lease and about 6 % of the history rows, all of them superseded within the last interval; a real network changes an address every few minutes at most.
4. **The readers of the derived rows.** The reconciler is woken by the writer's batch as it was by the lease batch: a change set of lease facts only, a light run that confirms the waiting moves (`reconcile/signal.rs`), now at most four a second instead of one per lease. The move step's waits that a lease fact ends are far above the writer's delay of at most 1.25 s: the verification window is 10 s at its minimum and 90 s by default, the wait for a client to come back 30 s, the extension window 15 s by default (`tests/lease_write_cost.rs` holds the ratio); an extension window set to its minimum of 1 s may see a client's last message a second late. The history API shows a change within the interval.
5. **What the lease row holds.** The row is unchanged: the fingerprint (WP8.2) and the DDNS state (WP8.3) stay in it, because the classifier reads the fingerprint when the change is built and the DDNS worker follows the lease rows. One lease commit writes 2 rows and 673 bytes of CBOR (the row about 560, the index entry and the batch the rest); the writer adds 11 rows and 3,048 bytes per change of the office shape (no name, OS or vendor facts for a client that sends none).
6. **Group commit with a bound.** The store's coalescer is the group commit: while an entry is made durable, the batches that arrive wait and go into the next one, and a lone batch at idle travels at once. Its bound was 1,000 batches and 8 MiB; it is now 128 batches and 64 KiB per entry (`tenko_store::raft::ENTRY_BOUND`), four entries in flight, so up to 512 batches in flight, the lease committer's own limit. A single larger batch still travels alone. The bound bites above about 128 batches per fsync, far above the M26 rates (at 10,000 per second and a 1 ms fsync, about 10 per entry): there the commits queue into the next entries instead of growing one.
7. **Snapshots by entries.** A snapshot is due after `snapshot_every_entries` (10,000) applied entries; `snapshot_every_log_mib` is a sanity bound sized to the entry, 1,024 MiB by default (10,000 entries of 64 KiB are 640 MiB; 64 MiB fired every 600 entries at 82 KB). The build streams a redb read transaction (an MVCC view) to a file on a blocking task beside the apply path, as before. A due snapshot is **deferred while the commit queue is deep**, more batches waiting for the coalescer than one entry takes, at most 30 s or until twice the entries are due (`snapshot_decision`); the deferral was chosen over a chunked build because the build already never holds the apply path and its only cost to the commits is the disk, which a build in chunks would spend all the same. The line `store snapshot built` gains `duration_ms`, `deferred` and `deferred_ms`. On one node the snapshot serves only the log's compaction; `snapshot_keep_entries` stays.
8. **Compression at the disk boundary.** The tables in memory keep their fixed layout. A Raft log record's entry is stored as raw DEFLATE at its fastest level when that is smaller (the high bit of the record's head length says so; older records read as before), and a snapshot file of format 2 is one DEFLATE stream after its header, its hash over the uncompressed bytes (format 1 still installs). The codec is `flate2` with its Rust backend, already a dependency of the workspace: zstd would compress somewhat better and faster but is not in the tree, needs a C library and a new licence review; the format leaves room for it. A compact on-disk encoding of the lease row would win the same bytes for the row alone and leave the facts, the journal and the snapshot as they are; compression is the simpler of the two.
9. **The state machine stays lazy, as it was.** The Raft log is the durability: every entry is fsynced in a group commit of the log writer before it commits (redb's one-phase commit with checksums: one `sync_data` per durable commit). The state machine applies without fsync (`[store] durability = "deferred"`, the default since WP1.1) and makes `sm.redb` durable in a checkpoint once a second and before every snapshot, with the last applied log id in the same transaction; a start replays the log from it. So a lease batch costs at most one fsync of the log, shared by every batch of its group (16 log commits for 1,000 concurrent lease commits in the cap test), and none of the state machine; the crash test kills the node between log commits and the next checkpoint nearly every time and loses no acknowledged lease.
10. **A background class in the coalescer.** `Store::propose_background` queues a batch nobody waits on now. The coalescer packs the foreground batches waiting first, in their order; an entry that carries any takes at most one background batch beside them, of at most 24 KiB (`BACKGROUND_SHARE`, about five changes of an office client), so a lease commit waits behind at most that share in the append and the apply. An entry without foreground batches carries background batches up to the entry bound, and a background batch that has waited 250 ms (`BACKGROUND_MAX_WAIT`) is packed as if none waited, so the class is never starved; the two classes keep no order between them. Measured on the workstation (release, lease commits paced with the writer beside them): at 1,000 a second a commit average of 5.6 to 6.8 ms and none above 100 ms (7.95 ms with 381 commits above 25 ms in the foreground); at 5,000 a second 11.4 to 13.2 ms, p99 41 to 60 ms, none above 100 ms, the writer's queue at most one interval and nothing dropped (32.9 ms, p99 216 ms, 1,248 above 100 ms in the foreground). The writer alone fills whole entries at 6,900 to 7,500 changes a second; beside the lease commits it gets one batch per entry and kept up at 5,000 a second here. The share was chosen at one batch of 16 KiB: one batch of 64 KiB per entry made the writer's apply the longest part of a lease entry.

The DNS update path has no amplification of its own: its rows are the RRsets, the journal entry IXFR needs and two counters; in a signed zone the signer's pass rewrites `dns.sign_state` (939 bytes) on every pass, a candidate for the next package. Its storms in the lab's M21 runs were the snapshot policy of point 7, which this record removes for both paths. On the Windows workstation the guard measured the same commit average before and after (main 9.2 and 8.9 ms at 1,000 per second, this package 9.1, 8.9 and 8.5; closed loop about 5,800 per second both), with the data directory's growth per lease down from about 8.0 to 5.3 to 7.0 KB: there the commit time sits on a floor of its own (a p50 of about 8 ms with an fsync p50 of 1.5 ms), not on the store work this record removes. The lab re-measures VM to VM at the gate against the figures of 2026-10-22; the gate guard (`bench/phase3-dhcp/scripts/guard.sh` with `bench/phase3-dhcp/guard.toml`) fails a change that costs commit time, closed-loop rate or store bytes per lease on the workstation.

## Consequences

- An acknowledged lease is as durable as before; its history row and facts appear within a second, and a crash inside that second loses the history rows of changes that a later change of the same address superseded. Tools that read `dhcp.history` right after a commit wait for the writer (`StoreLeases::derived().flush()` in-process; a short poll over the API).
- The store writes several times fewer bytes per lease, and its snapshots are rarer and smaller; the log and snapshot files are not readable by a version before this one (a cluster's members upgrade together; a single node reads its old files).
- The derived rows still cost 11 rows and 3 KB per change in `sm.redb`: an `ObservedFact` repeats what the lease row holds (the address, the MAC, the times). A leaner encoding of the lease facts is the next step if the state machine's apply becomes the cost.
- **The tail that remains.** The ACK-path runs match each commit above 25 ms with what ran while it waited: at 1,000 a second 87 to 98 % of them waited over the durable checkpoint of `sm.redb` (once a second), none over a snapshot build. redb keeps the state machine's pages in its write buffer between durable commits and writes them back and syncs them in the checkpoint, under its write lock, so the applies wait 25 to 75 ms on the workstation; a checkpoint every 100 ms or every 5 s moved the count little (89 and 43 slow commits in 10 s), and an fsync of every apply cost 30 ms per commit. It stays: the ACK waits for the apply only because openraft answers a write after it applies.
- **Next steps, out of this record:** (1) the derived rows themselves, 11 rows and 3 KB per change (a leaner fact row, or index rows kept in memory and rebuilt at start): 13 rows per lease cannot reach 10,000 leases a second with a complete history on one node, whatever the scheduling; (2) answering a lease commit when its log entry is durable rather than applied (DHCP-07 holds: the log is the durability), which takes the checkpoint stall off the ACK path; (3) if compression and the lazy state machine leave the Raft log as the cost, a sequential append-only log file instead of the redb-backed one (one append and one fsync per group, no B-tree pages).

## Alternatives considered

- **Keep the facts in the lease batch and make them smaller.** Two to six rows per commit stay on the ACK's path, and every lease commit still wakes the reconciler. Refused: the ACK should wait for what the client is promised and nothing else.
- **A persisted queue of the writer.** A second synchronous write per lease; see point 3.
- **Batch the facts in the IPAM reconciler instead of the role** (the role publishes leases, the reconciler derives facts). Moves the rules of `observe::ingest` away from the one writer of its source and makes a controller round trip part of the facts; refused for this package, a candidate when the data plane leaves the controller (Phase 5).
- **A fixed delay before a commit (dhcpd's delayed ACK as it is).** Delays a lone commit at idle; the coalescer's group commit gives the same batching under load without it.
- **A chunked snapshot build.** Spends the same disk; see point 7.
