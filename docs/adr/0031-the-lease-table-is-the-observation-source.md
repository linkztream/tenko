# ADR 0031: The lease table is the DHCP observation source

- Status: Accepted (the owner decided the package on 2026-10-31; the choices of this record within it are the builder's)
- Date: 2026-10-31
- Amends: ADR 0030 points 1 to 5, ADR 0019 point 3 and the plan's decision P3-Q8

## Context

[ADR 0030](0030-durability-classes-of-the-lease-path.md) took the derived rows of a lease change out of the batch the ACK waits for. The lab then measured the result VM to VM on 2026-10-31 (one worker, 20 s rungs, the node's fsync p50 0.3 ms). The ACK path is protected: the ceiling is 5,000 exchanges per second at one worker, 4,998 achieved with 0.04 % drops (0.0.8 lost 0.41 % at the 2,000 rung). Version 0.0.2 reached 9,990. The asynchronous writer, though, keeps up only to about 4,000 changes per second: at 5,000 it left 11,311 of 99,999 queued, and at 10,000 it dropped 85,603 of 135,000. The commit average at 1,000 per second is 7.2 ms against 2.6 ms for 0.0.2.

The cause is the work itself. Every lease change still writes 13 rows, 2 synchronous and 11 derived, and the node applies about 65,000 rows a second at most, and the writer shares that rate with the lease commits. Scheduling cannot raise it. The rows have to go.

What a lease change writes, and what each row holds:

| Row | Table | Holds |
|---|---|---|
| the lease | `dhcp.leases` | about 560 octets: scope, client transaction time, end, state, revision, node, the client, the names, the option 81 flags, the DDNS policy and outcome, the fingerprint, the partner times, the relay |
| the client index entry | `dhcp.leases.by_client` | the client's latest row in the scope |
| the history row | `dhcp.history` | the event and a copy of the client's identity and host name |
| an address fact | `ipam.obs.facts` and its index rows `ipam.obs.by_mac`, `ipam.obs.by_ip` | about 386 octets: source, source key, subject, subject key, payload, observation id, five timestamps, sightings, revision |
| a name fact | `ipam.obs.facts`, `ipam.obs.by_ip`, `ipam.obs.by_name` | the same shape |
| an OS fact and a vendor fact | `ipam.obs.facts` and index rows | the same shape |

The facts are a copy of the lease table in another shape. The address fact holds the address, the client's MAC address and the lease's times. The name fact holds the name the lease holds. The OS and vendor facts hold what the fingerprint in the lease row holds. Every renewal rewrote every fact row to move its last sighting and its sighting count forward. What a lease needs beyond the address, the MAC address and the time is the client identity, the state, the client's host name, the DDNS outcome and the history event, and all of it is in the lease row already.

## Decision

1. **DHCP writes no fact rows.** The lease tables, `dhcp.leases` and `dhcp.leases6`, are the observation source `dhcp:<node>`. The reconciler, the move step and every other reader of the facts of that source read the leases through a view that presents a lease as the facts it would have produced (an address, a name, an OS and a vendor fact), built on the in-memory index `LeaseFacts` (`tenko_ipam::observe::dhcp`). `ipam.obs.facts` and its index tables stay for the sources that have no table of their own: the probes, the network controllers, the zones' records. The rules of `observe::ingest` that decided when a fact is new or changed became the rules of the view: a lease row is the fact, its client transaction time is its last sighting, its grant time its first, a released, expired or reset row is not current, and an abandoned row stands for the conflict's address fact until its hold ends. The reconciler's light run is woken by the view as it was woken by the fact changes before.
2. **A lean, append-only history row.** `dhcp.history` keeps its key (time, VRF, address) and its API. Its value becomes the event, the state after it, the client identity, the scope, the node and the lease row's revision, about 60 octets, with no copy of the names, the fingerprint or the DDNS state. Those are in the lease row at that revision; the history read joins them from the lease row while it still has them and says where they came from. The row stays in the writer's background class; the retention and the purge are unchanged. The crash test's rule stands: an acknowledged lease's history row appears after the start's catch-up or is counted as lost by ADR 0030 point 3.
3. **The grant time in the lease row.** A new cold field `granted` holds the time the current binding was first granted. A renewal, a rebind or a repeated request of a live binding (the same client's active row whose end has not passed) keeps it; a release, a decline or an expiry of the same client keeps it on the ended row; a new grant after the binding ended (the same client's released or expired row, or an active row past its end) is a new binding and sets a new grant time, as does a grant to another client. Every other change sets it to the change's client transaction time. "Seen since" of the IPAM view and the move step's reasoning come from the row and not from the facts. A row without the field reads as granted at its client transaction time.

### Choices within the decision

a. **The view follows the store off the apply path.** `LeaseFacts` follows `Store::watch`, the stream the API's event stream reads, so it takes nothing from the store's apply path. It is built from the lease tables at start and again when the stream ends with `Lagged` or `Compacted` (a snapshot install). A reader that has a read view waits for the follower with `caught_up` (at most one second), so it sees the leases of its own read view. A reader whose wait times out reads the view as it is, a little behind its read view: the request never fails, and the wait is logged at debug with the lag in revisions. A reader without a follower builds the view from its own view. The view covers every node's rows, since the source of a fact is the row's node. It keeps one compact entry per lease row (185 octets inline, see the Consequences) and builds the facts when they are read.
b. **Existing `dhcp:*` fact rows are not read and are not removed at start.** The stored-facts source skips every fact of a `dhcp:*` source. The rows leave by the existing retention, 30 days after their last sighting. The retention purge reads `ipam.obs.facts` itself, not through the stored-facts source, so it does remove the old `dhcp:*` rows. Removing them at start would be a start-up write of up to four rows and their index rows per lease, on the data path; the retention already bounds them, and no reader sees them.
c. **No `dhcp:*` fact row is written again.** `observe::ingest` refuses observations of a `dhcp:*` source and counts them as skipped, and never expires the facts of such a source.
d. **The history row is a lean binary value.** It is a CBOR byte string, versioned by its first octet, with the event, the state after it and its end, the scope, the client identity, the node and the lease row's revision. The revision is absent when the history row is written in the lease row's own batch (the expiry and the operator's release). State, end and node are kept, about 10 octets, so that the history item's required fields hold for every row. Rows written by earlier versions (CBOR maps) still decode. The read joins the host name from the lease row and says which case it is in the item's `names`: `stored` (an old row holds the name itself), `lease` (the lease row is still at the event's revision), `lease_now` (the row changed since but belongs to the same client), `unknown` (another client holds the row or it is gone). `dhcp.history6` keeps the whole event in this change because of time: the lean codec and its join were built for the path the lab measures (E22 is DHCPv4), and the DHCPv6 rows are few against it. Its lean row is a next step.
e. **The grant time is additive.** `granted` is a serde field with a default and bit 12 of the presence mask of the role's in-memory row; the store's data format stays 5. The API's lease item gains `granted` (the grant time, else the client transaction time) and the history item gains `names`. The field `observed_at` is no longer written.
f. **The observation id of a lease fact is a hash.** It covers the source, the subject, the grant time and the attributes, so it changes when the binding or what it reports changes, as the ingest's id did. Sightings count one per refresh interval between the grant and the last transaction, so the adoption constraint `min_sightings` still has a count to measure. Two rows of one node that give the same subject (the name or the OS of one address in two VRFs, the vendor of one MAC address on two leases) give one fact: the current one, else the most recently seen.
g. **The reconciler's light run is woken by the view.** The view signals after it has taken a lease change in, so a move that waits for a lease is confirmed when the view has the lease. The change signal (`reconcile/signal.rs`) no longer needs to see the leases in the store's change sets.
h. **The view applies the observation retention.** A lease row whose facts ended (a released, expired or reset row at its client transaction time or its end, whichever came first; an active binding or an abandoned row at its end) longer ago than the observation retention is not presented by the view at all, so ghost addresses and "seen since" age out as they did with stored facts. The retention is the one the purge of the stored facts uses (`ObserveConfig::retention`, 30 days by default); the reconciler hands the setting in force to the view (`LeaseFacts::set_retention`) before every run and purge, and the build at start applies the same cut. The lease rows themselves are never deleted ([dhcp.md](../dhcp.md) section 8.4).

## Figures

The cap tests (`crates/tenko-dhcp/tests/lease_write_cost.rs`, 1,000 concurrent lease commits of the office shape on a Raft store in a temporary directory; a renewal is a new measurement) fail above their measured value plus 25 %. The figures are DHCPv4 only, except the last row:

| | before (ADR 0030) | after |
|---|---|---|
| rows in one lease commit | 2 | 2 |
| bytes of one lease commit's batch | 673 | 669 |
| log bytes per lease commit | 64 | 61 |
| derived rows per lease change | 11 | 1 |
| derived bytes per change | 3,048 | 125 bytes of CBOR in the batch (the history row's key and value are 85 octets; the cap test asserts them under 100) |
| the writer's log bytes per lease | 357 | 26 |
| a renewal | not measured | 1 lease row, no client index move, 1 history row; 555 bytes synchronous and 125 bytes derived |
| rows per lease (2 synchronous and the derived rows) | 13 (2 + 11) | 3 rows (2 and 1): the 2 rows of 0.0.2 plus the history row that 0.0.2 did not keep |
| derived rows per DHCPv6 change | up to 7 (the history row, the address fact and the name fact with their four index rows) | 1 (the history row, whole); not measured in bytes |

The lab measures VM to VM at the gate against the figures of 0.0.2: 9,990 exchanges per second at one worker and a commit average of 2.6 ms at 1,000 per second. The workstation guard (`bench/phase3-dhcp/guard.toml`, release builds, three runs on 2026-10-31) measured a commit average of 9.22, 9.00 and 9.38 ms at 1,000 per second (8.52 to 9.08 before, the floor of this machine), 5,083, 5,046 and 5,095 exchanges per second in the closed loop (5,576 to 5,989 in the runs of 2026-10-30), and 2,789, 2,961 and 2,931 bytes of store growth per lease (5,300 to 6,988); the byte threshold falls from 6,774 to 2,931. The closed-loop figure is about 12 % below the runs of 2026-10-30 and about 15 % below the 5,940 exchanges per second recorded for the previous change on 2026-10-31, and within the guard's tolerance; it was not measured against main on the same day, and the node now also runs the lease view's follower (a second reader of the journal and one read of each changed lease row) beside the role. The cause (the follower or noise) is not isolated and the lab's VM-to-VM run decides.

## Consequences

- **A reconciler that reads two shapes of source through one trait.** `FactSource` (`tenko_ipam::observe::source`) has two implementations, `StoredFacts` (`ipam.obs.facts` without any `dhcp:*` source) and `LeaseFacts`, and `Observations` joins them. The readers all go through it: the reconciler's snapshot (its runs, the adoption and the move step), the reconciliation view (the API, the health report and the findings), the observation API and the topology. None of them reads `ipam.obs.facts` or its index tables by itself for a lease. The probes' baseline reads stored facts only, because its inputs are probe facts.
- **The history API joins.** A history item carries the host name when the lease row still has it, and says so in `names`. A name that the lease row lost (another client holds the address now) is not known and is reported as `unknown`; the history row no longer carries a copy.
- **A `dhcp:*` fact row is never written again.** The metric `tenko_dhcp_lease_facts_total` is gone, with the hook that fed it (`IpamObservations`, `ObservationHook`). The writer of ADR 0030 point 2 carries the history row only, and the start's catch-up of point 3 rebuilds history rows only.
- **The view's memory replaces the role's index of facts.** `LeaseFacts` runs in the IPAM service of every node that runs the IPAM module (the controller role), leader or not, with or without the `dhcp` role: the reconciler's leader reads it, and so do the API, the findings and the topology on every controller. A node with the `dhcp` role alone has none, and the role itself keeps no index of facts any more (ADR 0030 point 2 kept one so that the role never read the store while it built a batch). Per lease row the view holds 185 octets inline (152 of entry and 33 of key, measured with `size_of` and asserted by the test `an_entry_of_the_view_stays_compact`), about 220 with the map's spare buckets, plus the host name, the vendor class and the OS guess on the heap: about 300 octets in all for an office client, so about 30 MB at 100,000 leases and about 300 MB at 1,000,000, on a controller. M30's 50 MiB bound is for the role's lease table on the `dhcp` node and is not touched.
- **ADR 0030 stands otherwise.** The two durability classes, the bounded group commit, the snapshots by entries, the compression at the disk boundary and the background class of the coalescer are unchanged. What changes is what the second class holds: the history row.
- **Existing fact rows stay until the retention removes them.** For up to 30 days after an upgrade the store holds fact rows of `dhcp:*` sources that nothing reads.
- **The data format stays 5.** Every change is additive: a field with a default in the lease row, a value shape of the history row that old rows do not have and that both shapes decode.
- **Observation ids change once at the upgrade.** A lease fact's observation id is now a hash, so every lease fact has a new id after the upgrade. Nothing breaks: a proposal's fingerprint, which decides a supersede and the rejection memory, covers the kind, the key, the evidence's source and subject keys, the MAC address, the IP address and the shape, not the observation ids. What an operator sees once: an open proposal's evidence shows the new ids after its next refresh, and the rows of `ipam.obs.last_seen` name the new ids at their next refresh (at most one write each, as any refresh). The shape of an orphaned-address proposal names the address's last sighting id, but an unseen address's sighting does not change, so such a proposal stays as it is.
- **Downgrade and mixed versions.** The data format stays 5 for the new binary, and the members of a cluster upgrade together, as in ADR 0030. An earlier binary cannot decode a lean history row: its history read of a page that holds such a row fails with a decoding error, and its start's catch-up logs it. A mixed-version cluster's older controller reads stored `dhcp:*` facts that nobody writes any more and misses the leases of the newer nodes.
- **Next steps, out of this record:** (1) the "last seen" table for renewals, in reserve: a table keyed by address that holds the client transaction time, the end and the partner times, so that a renewal leaves the lease row untouched when only the times change. It would change the contract of every reader of `ends` (expiry, the rebuild, the snapshot install, the lease API, the export). It is taken if the lab's VM-to-VM E22 ladder of this build stays below 9,000 exchanges per second at one worker (90 % of 0.0.2's 9,990) or above 4 ms of commit average at 1,000 per second (0.0.2: 2.6 ms); the owner decides on the figures; (2) the lean row for `dhcp.history6`, so that the DHCPv6 history is as small as the DHCPv4 one.

## Alternatives considered

- **A compact fact row per lease.** One row of a leaner shape instead of four, with its index rows. Refused: it is still a copy of the lease row that every renewal would rewrite, and the reconciler would read two shapes of the same truth.
- **The "last seen" table.** See the next steps in the Consequences. It leaves the lease row untouched on a renewal but moves the contract of every reader of the row's times; it is kept for the case the lab shows it is needed.
- **Keep the facts and schedule only.** This is what ADR 0030 did. Its measured ceiling: the writer kept up to about 4,000 changes a second (44,000 rows, 52,000 with the lease commits' own), against the node's ceiling of about 65,000 rows a second, which the writer shares with the lease commits. No scheduling raises it.
